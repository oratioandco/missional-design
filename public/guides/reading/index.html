Context around the passage

How much context does a reader need around the passage?

A daily reflection, a reference looked up mid-conversation and a sermon in preparation can all begin at Mark 3:5. The first wants one passage and some quiet. The second wants to land on the right verse. The third wants the whole chapter, two translations and the notes. The same verse needs three different screens.

01

Choose the reading unit by the task

A bounded passage suits a focused encounter: a devotional, a shared verse, a reflection that should be allowed to end. Continuous text suits sustained reading. Close study needs comparison, notes and room. None of these is the serious one; they serve different moments, sometimes for the same person on the same day.

Whatever the unit, keep wider context one deliberate step away. A bounded passage without a route to its chapter quietly decides how much of the story someone is allowed to see.

PatternA bounded passage with a route to wider context
02

Land in the right place, and keep it

When someone opens a reference, show where they have landed: scroll to the verse and mark it briefly. Keep that anchor when they change translation, open a footnote or come back from somewhere else. Losing someone’s place is one of the quickest ways to make a reading app feel careless.

PatternA reference opens at a deliberate destination

Getting to the passage in the first place is its own journey; see how someone finds the passage they are looking for.

03

Keep Scripture, notes and explanations distinct

Let people preview a note before a study panel takes over the screen. When notes appear, say whose they are: the translators’ footnotes, a publisher’s commentary and an AI-generated explanation carry different kinds of authority and should never look like the text itself.

Comparing translations works differently on a phone and a desk. A side-by-side layout that suits a laptop can crowd a small screen; alternating verses or a second pane on demand may serve better.

Take into your next review

What does this person need to understand or find, right now?

Exercise

Ask one person to open a shared passage, change translation, look at a footnote and return. Ask another to compare two editions while preparing to teach. Watch for lost places, labels people misread and context they went looking for and could not find.

Getting around easily is not yet evidence that anyone understood what they read; ask about that separately.
