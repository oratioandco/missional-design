Reflection for product work

Discernment

Make room for it: a product decision can be technically sound and still need a deeper conversation about the practice it encourages, the authority it claims and the people it serves.

Use one question where a decision contains a real tension. A chapter grid rarely needs an essay; a system that rewards devotion, interprets distress or monetises prayer needs more careful examination. Reflection should sharpen a decision and its accountability.

A short conversation before building

  • Purpose: What good are we hoping to serve? Is that also what the person came for?
  • Formation: What habits, expectations or dependencies might repeated use encourage? What makes that a hypothesis rather than an established outcome?
  • Authority: Whose interpretation is privileged? Can someone recognise, question and leave it?
  • Power: Who benefits from more activity, disclosure or spending? Whose experience is missing from the decision?
  • Freedom: Can a person pause, disagree, explore another route or finish without being treated as a failure?
  • Accountability: What would we change if the product met its targets but burdened the people it was intended to serve?

If prayer is part of your team’s practice

You might ask: what are we overlooking, whose experience should we listen to, and which organisational desire are we confusing with the person’s good? Leave room for silence and disagreement. Participation should be voluntary, especially where employment or funding creates pressure. Prayer does not replace consent, research, safeguarding, accessibility or a named person responsible for the decision.

Three readings that open the conversation

These selected perspectives do not represent every Christian tradition or settle the questions between them. They offer theological and pastoral arguments; their application to interface design below is editorial interpretation, not evidence of product effectiveness.

Discipline, grace and the person’s circumstances

In an interview, Dallas Willard discusses disciplines as context-sensitive means of practice rather than earning grace. The interviewer raises Reformation concerns about the relationship between practices, grace and corporate worship. That disagreement is worth retaining.

Design question: does a plan support a chosen discipline, or imply that completing it establishes a person’s spiritual standing?

Dallas Willard / Modern Reformation, Spiritual Disciplines and Means of Grace (2002). Theological interview; not a study of reading-plan outcomes.

Attention as listening to a neighbour

The Vatican’s Towards Full Presence discusses attention, silence and listening in digital life. Its account of listening extends beyond monitoring audience reactions. This Catholic pastoral perspective invites a richer question than whether content retained attention.

Design question: does the next notification make room for the person’s life, or mainly serve the organisation’s need to be noticed?

Dicastery for Communication, Towards Full Presence (2023), especially §§30–38. Reflection on social media; application to faith-app design is an adaptation.

Success and the pressure to demonstrate results

The evangelical Lausanne Cape Town Commitment calls for integrity in reporting and challenges demands for unrealistic visible results while retaining accountability. This offers a critique of success pressures from within an evangelical mission framework.

Design question: can we report subscriptions, reach and completion honestly without describing them as evidence of spiritual transformation?

Lausanne, The Cape Town Commitment, Part II E.4 (2010). A missional commitment; it does not establish which funding model or interface is best.

Bring the reflection back to the decision

Record the commitment you are making, a credible alternative, who bears the cost, and what evidence would prompt reconsideration. Teams can disagree about theology while making concrete commitments to honest communication, informed choice and responsible care.
