Attention and time

What would “enough for now” look like?

A reminder at 8 pm can help someone do what they meant to do. The same reminder, repeated by email, nudged again the next morning and followed by an endless feed, starts to work against them. Every app asks for attention; the question is whether it also knows when it has done its job.

01

Name the moment the app has done its job

Start with the practice you are supporting and the point at which it is complete. Longer sessions and more opens can mean value, confusion or compulsion; the number alone cannot tell you which.

Rewards make this harder to read. External rewards can weaken intrinsic interest even while participation rises, so a reminder someone chose, a learning challenge and a reward for opening the app every day deserve separate evaluation. Read the evidence on rewards, autonomy and habit.

Choose by what the person came for

03

Treat push, email and the app as one conversation

Offer choices by purpose, channel and frequency: a reading reminder by push, a weekly reflection by email, group replies in a digest, essential account messages separately. Allowing notifications in the operating system is not permission for every kind of marketing.

Don’t send the same prompt by push and email unless someone asked for both. Make the message useful on its own, with the reference, the edition and a link to the surrounding passage, and with a web fallback if the app isn’t installed. Put pause and unsubscribe in the message itself. Don’t read a tracking pixel as proof that someone read the passage.

Lock screens and shared inboxes can expose sensitive interests. Never build a preview from a prayer request, grief, an abuse disclosure or a private chat; even the sender’s name can reveal an affiliation.

04

Make the invitation honest

Avoid suggesting that God personally sent a notification, manufactured urgency, and anything that ties someone’s worth to opening the app. For some people, staying connected online is an important source of access and community, so “put the phone down” should remain an invitation, never an instruction.

Take into your next review

Would we still call it a good outcome if the person finished sooner, read the email without clicking, or took a week away?

Exercise

Compare a chosen reminder with no reminder, or with a lower frequency. Look at voluntary reports of usefulness, opt-outs, complaints about duplicate messages and finished tasks, alongside return rates, and include the people who stopped using the app. Don’t secretly track offline prayer to prove the product helped.

Platform guidance and regional checks

Apple’s subscription guidance requires opt-in and opt-out for marketing push and excludes sensitive information from those notifications; it is platform guidance, not universal law. The UK ICO’s electronic-mail guidance, updated in April 2026, includes a conditional soft opt-in for charitable purposes. Being a Christian organisation or a charity does not settle eligibility on its own; check the message, organisation, subscriber and conditions. Email tracking needs its own assessment.

These are source-specific requirements, not one universal notification law. See the regional research.

Where these proposals come from

The Kairos manuscript (Tobias Treppmann, Digital Spiritual Formation: The Aperto Vision, Draft 4, selected sections reviewed 5 October 2026) asks how formation goals can conflict with retention incentives. It is not an effectiveness study, and the full manuscript is not published here.
