Actions on AI answers

Copy, share, try again, report: which belong?

Under every answer, AI apps now offer a row of icons: copy, share, save, read aloud, try again, make it simpler, branch, double-check, thumbs up and down, report a legal issue. Each one makes a promise about where the answer goes, who sees the feedback and what the model did. In a Bible app, a shared answer can also be mistaken for Scripture. Each action is a chance to send people back to the text, or away from it.

1. Every action in a row

Borrowed from general AI apps: Gemini (good/bad, share and export, double-check, modify, report legal issue), ChatGPT (copy, read aloud, branch in new chat, try again) and Claude (copy, try again, thumbs with an issue type).

− Costs

2. Three, then More

Common in chat and content apps: the main actions visible, the rest in a menu with labels.

Try this

3. Actions on the passages

Observed in BibleAI, where each passage card has its own Bookmark and Share (see the citations page); generalized here.

+ Makes easier

− Costs

4. No action bar

Leave the bar out: the answer is just text, and people select it to copy.

+ Makes easier

A quieter screen with nothing to tap, which can suit reflective use.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Two or three actions, the rest behind More

    Ten unlabelled icons are a puzzle on a phone. Most people need two or three actions; the rest are easier to understand as labelled rows behind “More”.

  2. 2

    What travels matters most

    A copy or share should carry the label and the references, so whoever receives it can tell generated interpretation from Scripture or a pastor’s words. Making the passage, not the answer, the thing people save and share is stronger still.

  3. 3

    Changing the answer needs care

    Try again can become shopping for the doctrinal answer someone hoped for, so keep every version visible and the original first. Simpler and shorter versions help readers at different levels. Checking quotations against the edition catches misquoted verses but cannot judge the interpretation.

  4. 4

    Feedback and “thinking” need honesty

    Thumbs should say who reads them and whether they train models. A list of steps should describe what the service actually did, such as searching and checking quotations, not present the model’s reasoning as proof. A report button needs a named owner and a response time.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Holding answers lightly

  • Test, then keep what is good

    “Test all things. Hold fast to what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). Checking a quotation, comparing versions and reporting a problem are ways of testing; saving and sharing are ways of holding fast, and should carry what was tested.

Research and guidance

What each action promises

  • Icons need words

    Few icons are recognised by everyone, and labels revealed on hover do not work on touch screens. Label the actions people need, and put the rest in a labelled menu.

    DAurora Harley

  • Say what feedback does

    Human–AI interaction guidelines ask products to make clear what the system can do, how well, and what happens when people correct it. Thumbs that train a model and thumbs read by a pastor are different promises.

    SMicrosoft HAX ToolkitSGoogle

  • Regenerating can become shopping

    Assistants sometimes favour agreeing over accuracy; trying again until the answer agrees compounds it. Keep the original first and every version visible.

    SAnthropic researchersSITdaily

  • Checking changes trust

    People trust cited answers more, even with random citations, and less once they check. A check action that opens the verses builds the right kind of trust.

    RYifan Ding et al.

  • Overreliance is a known risk

    NIST lists overreliance among the risks of human–AI configuration. Actions that make an answer easier to keep and spread than to question add to it.

    SNIST

Where to start

Which fits your product?

  • IfAnswers are mostly read and occasionally kept

    Start withCopy, share and sources visible, everything else behind More

  • IfThe team depends on feedback to improve answers

    Start withKeep rating visible, and say clearly what happens to it

  • IfThe product exists to lead people into the text

    Start withPut bookmark and share on the passages instead of the answer

  • IfReflective use, with little to do afterwards

    Start withNo bar, but keep a way to report problems

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What doing is for

“Be doers of the word, and not hearers only” (James 1:22). Reading Scripture leads outward: to prayer, to obedience, to other people. The disciples on the road to Emmaus, their hearts burning, went straight back to Jerusalem to tell the others (Luke 24:32–33).

Where Christians differ

Christians share Scripture in many ways: verse cards, tracts, social media, a text to a friend. Some traditions encourage personal sharing; others prefer that teaching come through the church. All would distinguish the word of God from commentary on it.

What follows for design

“Let him who has My word speak it truthfully. For what is straw compared to grain?” (Jeremiah 23:28). Let the passage, not the generated text, be what people save and share, and label everything else. The best actions lead out of the app: read the whole passage, pray, talk to someone.

In conversation with

  • Albert Borgmann, Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life (1984) urged using technology to support focal practices rather than supplant them; the best action ends the session and begins the practice.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) saw Scripture as received in community; sharing a passage with a friend is closer to that than sharing generated text.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Generated text shared as if it were Scripture; prompts to keep chatting.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Label generated text and keep Scripture distinct from it.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Offer actions that lead away from the app: read the passage, pray, talk to someone.

Details

Go further

Edge cases to design for

Decide which actions this product needs. Keep two or three visible and put the rest behind “More”. Copy and share carry the AI label and references; saving to notes keeps AI answers apart from the reader’s own words; feedback says who sees it and whether it trains models; try again and simpler versions keep the original and let people compare, because regenerating until a preferred doctrinal answer appears is a real risk; checking quotations compares every quotation with the edition; “what the assistant did” lists real operations rather than private reasoning; reporting routes to a person with a stated response time. Consider putting actions on the Scripture objects instead, so the passage, not the generated text, is what travels.

What tends to go wrong

Icon-only rows without accessible names; a copy that drops the AI label; thumbs that silently feed training; “thinking” presented as proof of correctness; a report button with no stated owner; sharing generated interpretation as if it were Scripture.

How to test it

Ask people to say what each icon does before tapping it. Copy and share an answer and check what the recipient sees. Rate an answer and ask who they think will read the rating. Compare a full row with three actions plus “More” for findability and errors.

Faith and interpretation

What travels beyond the conversation matters. A shared answer may be read as Scripture, as the church’s teaching or as a friend’s counsel. Letting the passage, rather than the generated text, be the thing people save and share keeps the Bible and its interpretation distinct.
