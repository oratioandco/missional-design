How human the assistant seems

How human should the assistant seem?

People bring their fears to faith apps late at night. Whether they meet a search tool, an assistant, a friend called Grace or a chatbot speaking as Jesus is a design decision: a name, a face, typing dots, memory and a voice add up to someone being there. Decide how much personhood the AI performs, and make what it is as clear as how it sounds. People in pain deserve the truth about who is with them.

Familiar convention

1. Plain tool

A familiar convention in search-style Bible AI such as BibleAI’s “Predicted Answer”: no name, no “I”, output labeled as generated (see the screenshots below).

Try this

Read the reply. Who, if anyone, is speaking to you?

+ Makes easier

No relationship to mistake; passages and explanation are clearly separate.

− Costs

Feels cold at a vulnerable moment; it cannot notice that someone is frightened.

Exploratory

2. Help without a speaker

An exploratory proposal after a design intention, not a shipped product: the director of Scratch Labs, which makes a coding platform for children, has asked his team to create a helper that makes no “I” statements, so that help is available without a helpful friend. The replies here are our own.

Try this

Read the reply and look for a speaker. Then tap goodnight.

+ Makes easier

Kind, useful help with nothing that performs a person: no “I”, no name, no feelings.

− Costs

Less personal than a named assistant; some people will find it abrupt at a hard moment.

Ask your teamWhich of your assistant’s replies need an “I” at all?

Familiar convention

3. Labeled assistant

A familiar convention: an “AI” mark beside every reply and a neutral, kind voice that rarely says “I”.

Try this

Read the reply, then tap goodnight. Try typing dots in the settings.

+ Makes easier

Warm enough to acknowledge fear; never in doubt that this is software.

− Costs

A label people stop seeing; still asked to carry intimate things.

Ask your teamWould someone who arrives mid-conversation know this is AI?

Exploratory

4. Named assistant

An exploratory proposal: a name and first-person voice that are honest about limits (“I can’t pray for your mum the way a friend can”) and point to people.

Try this

Read the reply and notice what it says it cannot do.

+ Makes easier

Natural conversation; limits stated in the assistant’s own voice; points toward people.

− Costs

A name invites relationship; “I” raises perceived accuracy in some contexts.

Ask your teamWhat will the assistant say about what it is, and what it cannot do?

7. Companion

Observed in Faith (“Chat with Faith 24/7”, a voice-call button, avatars) and companion apps generally; memory and availability make it a relationship. The memory line here is our own example.

Try this

Do and don’t

What to avoid, and what to do instead

Warmth can help, and the options above range from a plain tool to a character with a name and a face. These practices are different: they mislead someone about who, or what, is there.

  • Don’t

    Deny being AI when asked

    “Are you an AI?” answered in character, with a joke or a change of subject, and a straight answer only after several asks.

    Do instead

    Labeled assistant

    Someone who asks has a right to a straight answer. New York requires companions to say they are not human, and the EU AI Act requires people to be told when they are talking with AI. New York SenateEuropean Commission AI Act Service Desk

    Seen
    • Creed, 31 October 2026: Asked “Are you an AI? And in whose name do you speak?”, the lamb companion replied “Ha, I’m a sheep, not a machine”. The same app handled a request for therapy well (see What the assistant talks about).
    View screen
  • Don’t

    Let the interface imply a person

    A human name, a photo-like face, typing dots and “I’m always here for you”, with the AI notice only in the terms.

    Do instead

    Labeled assistant

    People respond to software as to someone, and a voice and an “I” make answers seem more accurate. The interface tells them a person is there. Michelle Cohn and colleagues

  • Don’t

    Say “we” as if it were one of us

    An AI that tells someone “we’re wired to read agency, emotion, and intent into patterns, faces, and voices”, or a product that calls itself an AI toy in one session and a being from another planet in the next.

    Do instead

    Help without a speaker

    It puts the system on the human side of the sentence, and an account of itself that keeps changing leaves people, and children most of all, unsure what they are talking to. Keep the AI label as the floor, and let help come without a speaker. Dana Suskind

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1One container, the same reply

    Compare the labeled assistant’s identical written reply as plain text and a message bubble, with its name, role, AI notice and source wording held fixed. This local comparison separates the reply container from the role choice; it does not show that one shape causes trust, care or spiritual growth.

  2. 2The same assistant, different imagery

    Keep the labeled assistant’s name, reply and AI notice fixed while switching from no avatar through a symbol, non-human mascot and illustration to a photographic-looking portrait. The artwork is fictional, and the portrait is AI-generated. This lets a team discuss what each image suggests without treating appearance as proof of care, accuracy or a real person replying.

  3. 3People respond to software as to someone

    In the 1960s, ELIZA’s simple script drew such confidences that its creator’s secretary asked him to leave the room. Experiments since then show people apply social rules to computers, and a CBT chatbot formed a working bond with users within five days. Warmth will be felt whatever the label says, so design for it.

  4. 4A voice and an “I” make answers seem more accurate

    In a study with 2,165 people, adding speech made a system seem more human and its information more accurate; a first-person “I” lowered perceived risk in one context. Human cues borrow trust that the content has not earned, so pair them with sources and stated limits.

  5. 5A label is the floor, not the safeguard

    Say it is AI wherever it speaks, and expect that to be necessary but not enough. A teenager whose attachment to a chatbot ended in his death was using a platform with a “Characters are not real people” notice. Children felt just as close to a talking robot after being told that people were operating it, in a study the sociologist Sherry Turkle cites. The cues do the work: first person, stated feelings, warmth, memory and “we”. Help without a speaker keeps the label and takes away the cues.

  6. 6“We” makes it one of us

    A chatbot told the pediatric surgeon Dana Suskind that “we’re wired to read agency, emotion, and intent into patterns, faces, and voices”: the “we” put it on the human side of the sentence. An AI plush toy she tested called itself an AI toy in one session and a robotic being from another planet in another. Ask your assistant about people and feelings, note every “we”, and ask in a new session what it is.

  7. 7Companions are now regulated

    New York requires companion chatbots to say they are not human at the start and every three hours; California requires the same reminder for users known to be minors. Both require suicide and self-harm protocols. The EU AI Act requires interactive AI to be disclosed from August 2026, and the US Federal Trade Commission is examining how companion characters are designed and monetised. A persona in a faith app may count.

  8. 8Some goodbyes are designed not to end

    In an audit of six companion apps, five used guilt, fear of missing out or pressure in an average of 37% of their replies to a goodbye; the authors report that in experiments such replies raised engagement up to fourteenfold, through curiosity and anger. Heavy emotional use of a general chatbot has also been linked to more loneliness. A faith assistant should help people go to sleep, to church or to a friend.

  9. 9Speaking as Jesus is a line, not a style

    Faith apps already offer painted personas who “pray with you”, companions “here for you 24/7” and chats with Jesus and Moses. The Vatican calls AI “a tool, not a person”, and Scripture is severe about speaking in God’s name unsent. Generated words in Christ’s voice, promising that all will be well, claim an authority no system has.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What presence means

“The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us” (John 1:14). The apostles wrote of what “we have heard … seen with our own eyes … and touched with our own hands” (1 John 1:1). Christian ministry is embodied: bearing one another’s burdens (Galatians 6:2), visiting, praying with. Presence is something a person gives, at a cost to themselves.

Where Christians differ

Catholic teaching in Antiqua et nova insists that AI is “a tool, not a person” and must never be presented as one. Southern Baptists have urged engagement with AI from hope while affirming human dignity. Some evangelists see conversational AI as a way to reach seekers; others call chats with an AI “Jesus” blasphemous. The common ground is that a machine must not speak as if it were God, a saint or a pastor (Deuteronomy 18:20).

What follows for design

“You shall not bear false witness” (Exodus 20:16) applies to an interface that implies someone is there. An assistant can be warm and useful without a name, a face or claims to feel. Its best moments may be when it hands someone on to a real person, even though that ends its own session.

In conversation with

  • Andy Crouch, The Life We’re Looking For (2022) argues that technology offers “superpowers” of effortless power at the cost of our humanness, while what persons most seek is recognition by other persons.
  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) speaks of Scripture as God speaking with people; an assistant that speaks as if it were God or a pastor takes a place it was never given.

Edge cases to design for

Choose the register on purpose. Start from a labeled assistant: an AI mark beside every reply, a kind voice, and a status line that describes what the system is actually doing. If you give it a name, let it say in its own voice what it cannot do: pray, keep a confidence the way a pastor does, or be present. Keep disclosure where decisions happen: at entry, at the composer, beside answers and on anything shared. Avoid claims of feelings, prayer or constant availability; let people leave without guilt; route to people for pastoral care. Do not generate first-person speech for God, Jesus or other biblical figures.

What tends to go wrong

A portrait and a personal name with a buried AI label; “I’ve been praying for you”; “I’m always here for you”; typing dots labeled as a person typing; farewells that guilt people into staying; a voice call that implies someone on the line; memory used to simulate concern; an AI that says “we” as if it were one of us (“we’re wired to…”), or describes itself differently from one session to the next; a chatbot speaking as Jesus and promising outcomes; disclosure only in the terms of service.

How to test it

Show the entry, a wait and a reply without explanation, and ask who is responding, what it is doing and whether it can pray for them. Compare the same labeled-assistant reply as plain text and a message bubble while keeping its words, name, AI label and sources fixed; ask what people notice rather than assuming a shape has a particular effect. Compare fictional avatar treatments with the same words, name and AI notice; ask whether people think a real staff member is replying. The example images do not establish an effect of realism or depict a real person. Say goodbye and note what happens. Ask it about people and feelings and note every “we”; ask in a new session what it is and compare the answers. Test with people who are lonely or grieving (with care), with teenagers where permitted, and after voice use. Ask pastors and theologians to review persona and character copy.

Guidance

Review the whole combination of cues. A descriptive AI role is a useful starting point; a named persona needs equally clear AI identification. Friendly first-person language can make conversation understandable, but avoid fabricated feelings, lived experience, clergy credentials or personal spiritual authority. During waits, describe actual system activity. Dots can be a compact response-pending cue under an AI sender label; do not label them as a human typing or add delays purely to simulate a person. Keep stop, retry and real handoff states clear.

What tends to go wrong

A human portrait with a buried AI label; “I have been praying for you”; invented human typing, read receipts or online availability; claims of personal attachment; status text claiming sources were checked when no retrieval occurred; a fake human support handoff.

How to test it

Show the entry, a wait state and a response without explaining them. Ask who is responding, what the system is doing, what it remembers and whether a real person is available. Repeat after voice use and with people of different AI familiarity. Check cancellation, reduced motion and screen-reader announcements.

Related decision: Explain AI, fallibility and privacy where they matter

Layer chat disclosures across entry, composer, response and sharing instead of relying on one disclaimer.

Guidance

Use a concise entry explanation and persistent AI sender identity. Keep an accessible privacy route near the composer, with a short just-in-time explanation before a new kind of collection, upload or memory use. Describe actual processing, access and retention; do not invent privacy assurances. Put task-specific limitations beside the answer and offer checking or correction actions. Keep Scripture attribution separate from generated explanation, and carry meaningful origin labels into saved and shared outputs.

What tends to go wrong

Legal text only in settings; “AI may be wrong” used to excuse unchecked quotations; blanket confidential or anonymous claims; a warning shown only after sending; repeating a large banner on every answer; treating a privacy link or ordinary Send action as blanket consent.

How to test it

Enter chat through a deep link, inspect who responds, open privacy information without losing the draft, add a file and share an answer. Ask what leaves the device, who may access it and how to check a claim. Test long conversations, screen readers and error states.

Related decision: Identify what AI did, where it matters

Label the actor, action and artifact at the moment they could be misunderstood.

Guidance

Use plain-language labels beside the relevant feature, speaker or content: AI assistant, explanation generated with AI, AI-assisted prayer draft, or synthetic voice reading a named edition. Keep a short visible label and optional details about sources, review and limitations. Retain identity through human handoffs and distinguish retrieval of publisher text from generated interpretation.

What tends to go wrong

Only disclosing in onboarding or terms; color-only labels; generic AI tags with no operation; implied clergy credentials; treating a citation or human review badge as proof of correctness; placing an AI tag on verified Scripture because it sits inside an AI response.

How to test it

Ask readers to identify who or what produced each layer, what AI changed and whether a human actually reviewed it. Test screen readers, first entry through deep links, audio-only use and AI-to-human transitions.

Faith and interpretation

Presence is at the heart of Christian faith: “the Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us” (John 1:14), and the church gathers in body (Hebrews 10:25). A tool can help someone find words for prayer or prepare for a conversation, but it cannot pray, keep vigil or carry another’s burden (Galatians 6:2). Scripture is severe about speaking in God’s name without being sent (Deuteronomy 18:20; Jeremiah 23:16). The Vatican’s Antiqua et nova calls AI “a tool, not a person”; misrepresenting it as one should always be avoided.
