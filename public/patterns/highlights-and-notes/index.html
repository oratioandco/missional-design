Highlights and notes

How should readers mark a passage?

Readers mark Scripture to come back, to remember, to ask a question or to talk with others. Every mark says something and is kept somewhere: a ribbon says only “return here”, a highlight says “this”, a note says what the reader thinks, and a shared note says it to others. Decide how much a mark asks, how it looks, and who can see it. A mark is a small act of attention, sometimes of prayer. Treat what people mark as theirs.

What we noticed

Takeaways

  1. Reading with a pen is old

    Readers have marked Bibles for centuries; Jonathan Edwards filled an interleaved Bible with over five thousand notes. A study of used textbooks found placemarks, signals, interpretation and problem working, and students sought out well-annotated copies. Support several kinds of mark, not one palette.

  2. Highlighting is for returning, notes are for remembering

    A review of study techniques rated highlighting low for learning, and decades of studies show people remember what they put in their own words. That does not make highlights pointless: they say “come back here”. Offer a note one tap away, never as a requirement.

  3. Colour cannot carry the meaning alone

    Marks, selection, search and read-along highlighting often share the same tint. Give each label a shape (underline, outline, wavy line) and show playback with a different cue, so the reader can tell a saved mark from a moving one without relying on colour.

  4. A mark belongs to a translation, a note to the verse

    A highlight on the BSB’s “Be still!” has no exact match in the KJV’s “Peace, be still.” Keep exact-word marks with their translation and say so when the reader is elsewhere; let bookmarks and notes follow the verse.

  5. Notes on Scripture are sensitive

    Notes record faith, doubt, grief and sin. Under European data law, religious belief is special-category data. Keep notes private by default, make sharing a separate step that names the audience, and label other readers’ notes as theirs, as Glorify’s Public Notes switch begins to do.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What keeping the word is for

Scripture asks to be kept: written “on the doorposts of your houses” (Deuteronomy 6:9), hidden in the heart (Psalm 119:11), pondered as Mary “treasured up all these things” (Luke 2:19). The blessed person meditates on the law “day and night” (Psalm 1:2). A mark is often a prayer, a question or a promise held onto in a hard week.

Where Christians differ

Some traditions treat the physical book with great reverence: the Gospel book is carried in procession and kissed, and some believers would not write in a Bible. Others have read with a pen for centuries: Jonathan Edwards filled an interleaved Bible with thousands of notes. Traditions also differ on how personal interpretation relates to the church’s reading (2 Peter 1:20).

What follows for design

A mark belongs to the reader. Keep it private unless they choose otherwise, let it travel with them if they leave, and never mine it for advertising. Make marks distinguishable from the text itself, so a reader’s note is never mistaken for Scripture.

In conversation with

  • Eugene Peterson, Eat This Book (2006) commends lectio divina, dwelling on a short passage until it is taken in; a mark is often the trace of that dwelling.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) pairs life together with time alone with Scripture: what a reader keeps privately belongs to that time alone.

Edge cases to design for

Offer a small menu over the selection with one-tap marking and undo; leave labels and organisation for later. Give each kind of mark its own shape, and keep selection, search and playback cues visibly different from saved marks. Attach highlights to the exact words in their translation and say so elsewhere; attach bookmarks and notes to the verse so they follow any translation. Keep notes private by default and treat them as sensitive data. Make sharing a separate step that names the audience and previews what they will see; label other readers’ notes as theirs and let people turn them off.

What tends to go wrong

A colour picker before every highlight; colour as the only difference between marks; read-along highlighting that looks like a saved mark; highlights silently lost or moved onto different words after switching translation; notes public by default; a “share” that publishes to everyone; others’ notes styled like study notes; inferring mood or belief from marks for analytics.

How to test it

Mark, undo and re-mark a phrase; repeat with a screen reader and in greyscale. Switch translation and ask where each mark went and why. Play audio over marked verses. Ask people who can see a note they just shared, and compare with the truth.

Earlier pattern: The annotation anchor is explicit

Make exact wording and reference-based notes different personal objects.

Guidance

Allow quick marking without forced organisation. Explain whether the annotation targets an exact edition-specific selection or a passage reference. Colours and labels are optional personal conventions.

What tends to go wrong

Assuming a colour reveals theology or mood; requiring tags before saving; promising exact word transfer across translations.

How to test it

Can users understand where their note will appear after switching editions? Test unlabelled highlights and annotations across verse ranges.

Earlier pattern: Overlaps remain legible

Distinguish playback, selection, search and saved highlights.

Guidance

Use different visual and accessible cues for transient and saved states. Carry reference-based notes through reliable reference mappings. Keep exact selections attached to their original edition when no word alignment exists.

What tends to go wrong

Everything represented by background colour; inventing a new selection in another edition; hiding the original annotation when mapping fails.

How to test it

Combine playback, search and a personal mark on one verse. Switch editions and check that every annotation remains explainable and recoverable.

Earlier pattern: Mark a passage without interrupting the thought

Quick highlighting and deeper organisation can happen at different moments.

Guidance

Offer a small selection menu with Highlight, Note and More. Apply a sensible last-used or default style with undo. Make style labels and organisation optional, preserve the selection anchor, and provide an accessible selection path beyond long press.

What tends to go wrong

Marking the wrong range; gesture-only selection; requiring a colour taxonomy; confusing an audio cue with a saved mark; losing marks on edition changes.

How to test it

Select a range crossing paragraphs, apply and undo a mark, add a note, then switch editions. Check narrow screens, handles near screen edges, keyboard access and save feedback.

Earlier pattern: Keep my note distinct from a shared comment

Origin, audience and anchor belong to the note—not just its screen.

Guidance

Start personal notes as private. Show the passage or exact-text anchor and save state. Make sharing a separate previewed action with an explicit audience. Keep publisher commentary and AI suggestions visibly separate from the person’s own words.

What tends to go wrong

A public composer styled like a private journal; changing visibility without confirmation; silent sync failure; quoting someone else’s disclosure into a shared thread.

How to test it

Create a private note offline, inspect its anchor and preview sharing with a group. Cancel sharing, change edition and find the original note. Check what deletion can and cannot remove from recipients’ copies.
