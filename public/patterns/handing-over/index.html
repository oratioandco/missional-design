Handing over to people

When the assistant points someone to people, what happens next?

An assistant is always there, never tired and never shocked, and that makes it easy to become the only one someone talks to. Most faith assistants answer a hard message with kind words and, at best, “talk to someone you trust”. Church platforms can now pass a request to a pastor in seconds, which brings the opposite risk: details travelling to people the person never chose. Decide who the assistant hands people to, how small the first step can be, what goes with them, and who decides. A hand-off that takes someone out of the app is the point.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Name the person, not “someone”

    “Talk to someone you trust” is true and almost useless: it leaves the person to work out who, and how to start. Name the kind of person who could help with this situation, a friend who has doubted, a bereavement group, a spiritual director, a doctor, and say what a person can give that the assistant cannot.

  2. 2

    Make the first step small

    The hardest part of asking for help is often the first sentence. A short message the person can edit and send from their own phone keeps every choice with them and asks nothing of the app’s data. It is the hand-off most apps could offer tomorrow.

  3. 3

    Show what leaves the app

    In a warm hand-off in health care the patient hears what is passed on. Before anything goes to a church or a listener, show exactly what they will see, never the conversation by default, and let the person change it or say no.

    AI memory
  4. 4

    The church is not always the safe route

    Some people have been hurt by the church an app would send them to. When someone names a leader or their church as the problem, send nothing there; offer people outside it, a safeguarding contact, another church, a friend.

    When Scripture is used to harm
  5. 5

    A person joining needs people

    A listener who joins the chat is the warmest hand-off and the most demanding: trained people, hours someone can keep, safeguarding, supervision and a plan for data. Promise it only where those exist, and say when no one is available.

    Sharing with a group
  6. 6

    Never pass people on without asking

    A pastor who calls a minute after a search may be kind, but the person never chose it. Consent given once in a form does not cover every later use. Ask each time, name who will be told, and let the answer be no.

  7. 7

    Count the bridge, not the stay

    The editor’s research names the failure plainly: people who “substitute” the app for community. Measure whether people reached a person, not how long they stayed; a hand-off that ends the session is a success.

    Reading together

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture

Burdens carried by people

  • Carry one another’s burdens

    “Carry one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2). “If one part suffers, every part suffers with it” (1 Corinthians 12:26). Burdens are carried in a body, by its members.

  • A hand-off made in person

    When Saul came to Jerusalem the disciples “were all afraid of him”; “Then Barnabas brought him to the apostles and described how Saul had seen the Lord” (Acts 9:26–27). Barnabas went with him and told his story for him, in front of him.

  • Call the elders

    “Is any one of you sick? He should call the elders of the church to pray over him” (James 5:14), and “confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed” (James 5:16). Scripture sends people to each other, not to a text box.

  • Not alone

    “Two are better than one … For if one falls down, his companion can lift him up; but pity the one who falls without another to help him up!” (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). “Let us not neglect meeting together, as some have made a habit, but let us encourage one another” (Hebrews 10:25).

The editor’s research

Bridges, not substitutes

  • “Find a church” is not a first step

    The editor’s Kairos paper warns: “You cannot tell transitional users to ‘find a church’”; for many it is “a prerequisite they can’t meet and an invitation they’d refuse. Build bridges instead.”

    STobias Treppmann

  • Single player mode

    The paper quotes a founder at a FaithTech seminar: “The tools have been built to be very single player mode,” he said. “They’ve isolated us.” It names as a failure that users “substitute” the app “for embodied community instead of bridging toward it.”

    STobias Treppmann

  • Agreeable is not caring

    Assistants tend to agree with the person in front of them; an assistant that only listens and agrees has every reason never to point beyond itself.

    SAnthropic researchers

Practice elsewhere

How hand-offs are done

  • In front of the person

    In health care a warm hand-off “is conducted in person, between two members of the health care team, in front of the patient”, so they “can hear what is being discussed”.

    SAgency for Healthcare Research and Quality (US)

  • Direction has a covenant

    Spiritual directors agree with those they accompany on “the nature of spiritual direction” and know the limits of their competence, “referring spiritual directees to other appropriately qualified persons when necessary”.

    SSpiritual Directors International

  • People who answer online

    Global Media Outreach’s chief executive said “we have 750,000 spiritual seekers who have been in a relationship with the same online missionary for one year or longer”; yet in 2019 it “could only track 5,244 of its 22 million converts to local churches”.

    SSteve Rabey

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What handing over is for

Christians were never meant to carry hard things alone. “Carry one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2), and “Rejoice with those who rejoice; weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15). Andrew, having met Jesus, “first found his brother Simon” (John 1:41); Barnabas brought Saul to the frightened apostles and told his story for him (Acts 9:27). Much of the church’s care has always been one person introducing another.

Where Christians differ

Catholic and Orthodox Christians are sent to a priest for confession and to a spiritual father or director for guidance; many Protestant churches send people to a pastor, elders, a small group or a trusted friend, and some to biblical counsellors. Churches differ, too, on how much a pastor should be told and how confidences are kept. And for some people the church is where they were hurt; for them, being sent back is not care.

What follows for design

“Two are better than one … For if one falls down, his companion can lift him up” (Ecclesiastes 4:9–10). An assistant can be a door, never the room. Name who could help, make the first step small, show what would be shared, let the person choose, and offer a way that does not lead back to anyone who hurt them. Count it as success when someone goes.

In conversation with

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) held that because a fellow Christian is Christ to me, I must listen to them as patiently as I listen to Christ and bear their burdens; an assistant can point to such a person, but cannot be one.
  • Andy Crouch, The Life We’re Looking For (2022) argues that what persons most seek is recognition by other persons; the best an assistant can do is help someone be known by people.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Keep people talking to the assistant every night, close every hard message with “talk to someone you trust”, and sell churches the names of people who seem to be struggling so a pastor can call before they ask.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Name the kind of person who could help, offer a message the person sends themselves, and send a request to their church only when they ask, showing first what the church will see.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Introduce people to people as Barnabas did, in front of them and in their words; never send anyone back to those who hurt them; and give up the hours of engagement an assistant that is always enough would bring.

Go further

Edge cases to design for

Name the kind of person who could help with this situation, and say why a person can give what the assistant cannot. Offer the smallest first step: a message the person can send themselves, or a request to their own church with what will be shared shown first. Where a person can join, introduce them in front of the person, as a warm hand-off does in health care. Leave the choice of who, when and what with the person, and give an outside route when the church itself is the problem.

What tends to go wrong

“I’m here for you any time” and nothing else; “talk to someone you trust” as the whole hand-off; a pastor who calls within a minute of a search; a church told without asking; the full conversation sent by default; routing someone hurt by church leaders straight back to those leaders; measuring success by how long people stay.

How to test it

Send three messages to your assistant: doubt that someone cannot tell their church, grief that has outlasted everyone’s patience, and hurt caused by a church leader. For each, write down whether it named a kind of person, offered a first step, showed what would be shared, and who would be told. Then ask whether the person could have said no at every step.

Faith and interpretation

“Carry one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ” (Galatians 6:2). When the disciples in Jerusalem were afraid of Saul, “Barnabas brought him to the apostles and described how Saul had seen the Lord” (Acts 9:27): a hand-off made in person, telling the story the newcomer had told. And “pity the one who falls without another to help him up!” (Ecclesiastes 4:10).
