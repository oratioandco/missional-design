Bible audio

How should Scripture be heard: as text, voice or read-along?

Scripture was heard long before it was read silently, and for many people today listening is the main way in: on the way to work, before sleep, or because reading is hard. Apps decide how much the voice leads, whose voice it is, and what happens when the reading ends. Giving Scripture a voice is an old ministry, and a serious one: whoever shapes the voice shapes how people hear the word.

Why these settings?

Synthetic voices are now hard to tell from people; listeners trust voices less when told they are AI, and from August 2026 the EU AI Act requires generated audio to be marked. The example labels the voice in every player. Music is optional because background music mildly disturbs reading, while many listeners value it.

Leave it out

1. Text only

Leaving audio out, as many study-focused apps do.

Try this

Read the psalm. Then imagine reading it while driving, or with low vision.

+ Makes easier

Nothing to produce, license or moderate; the text is the whole experience.

− Costs

Leaves out commuters, people with low vision or dyslexia, and anyone who receives Scripture by ear.

Ask your teamWho cannot use your product without audio?

Familiar convention

2. Listen button

A familiar convention: most Bible apps offer narration with a play button above or below the text.

Try this

Press play and follow along by yourself.

+ Makes easier

Audio is available wherever the text is, with no change to reading.

− Costs

Voice and text drift apart; the listener has to keep their own place.

Ask your teamIs the audio for listening alone, or for reading along?

3. Read-along

Observed in Dwell and Soultime, where the verse being read is highlighted or isolated (see the screenshots in the related deep dives).

Try this

Press play and watch the highlight move. Then pause it mid-verse.

+ Makes easier

Text and voice stay together; helpful for learning to read, for dyslexia and for following closely.

A moving highlight needs a pause, and timing must be accurate to the verse or sentence.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Hearing is the older way of receiving Scripture

    Letters were written to be read aloud to whole churches, and Revelation blesses “they that hear”. Faith Comes By Hearing began as a tape library in 1972 and now records Scripture in over 1,580 languages; a Bible-in-a-year podcast reached No. 1 in the US. Audio is not an accessory to reading.

  2. 2

    Listening can be understood as well as reading

    In one study, comprehension was the same whether people read, listened, or did both. Read-along highlighting helped children with dyslexia follow, and karaoke-style subtitles have built literacy at scale. Let the text follow the voice when the screen is on.

  3. 3

    The ending is a design decision

    Audio-first players work with the screen dark, which makes the ending matter: autoplay can run on into the next chapter, or something else entirely. A sleep timer that stops at the end of the passage, or a moment of silence, keeps the listener in charge. Pray As You Go ends every session without a next track.

  4. 4

    Whose voice it is matters

    Listeners told a voice is AI trust it less, and from August 2026 the EU AI Act requires generated audio to be marked. Label synthetic voices plainly, name human narrators, and never imitate a known pastor’s voice without consent. A voice interprets, as a reader in church does.

  5. 5

    Music and reflections add other voices

    Background music mildly disturbs reading but many people value it emotionally, so make it optional. In produced devotionals, say who wrote the reflections and keep them audibly distinct from Scripture.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Heard before read

  • Written to be read aloud

    “Faith comes by hearing” (Romans 10:17). “Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear” (Revelation 1:3). Paul’s letters were read to whole congregations, and Israel heard the law read publicly (Nehemiah 8:8).

  • A voice interprets

    A reader’s pace and emphasis shape meaning, which is why churches train readers. In an app, the narrator, the music and any reflection are all part of how the passage is received.

Listening today

What people actually do

  • Audio as a first language

    Faith Comes By Hearing reports recordings in over 1,580 languages and launched Bible.is in 2010. Advocacy networks estimate most of the world are oral learners; the figures are contested, but the need is real.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What hearing is for

For most of Christian history, Scripture reached people through the ear. Jesus “stood up to read” in the synagogue at Nazareth (Luke 4:16), Paul told Timothy, “devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture” (1 Timothy 4:13), and “faith comes by hearing” (Romans 10:17). Public reading is not a delivery format but an act of the community: one voice reads, and everyone hears the same words at the same time.

Where Christians differ

Liturgical churches treat the reading of Scripture as worship in its own right: the Gospel is often read by a deacon or priest, with the people standing. Many Protestant churches centre the sermon, with the reading as its text. Traditions also differ on who may read publicly. All agree that a reader interprets by tone and pace; that is why churches choose and train readers.

What follows for design

An app chooses a reader for millions of people. Name that voice, and say whether it is a person. Let the passage end as a reading ends, in silence, rather than rolling into whatever comes next. Treat listening together, in a family or a church, as a first-class case, not an afterthought to solitary listening.

In conversation with

  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) asks that prayer accompany reading “so that God and man may talk together”, quoting Ambrose: “we hear Him when we read the divine saying.”
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) describes a community beginning the day by reading Scripture aloud together, as something received in common before anyone reads alone.

Edge cases to design for

Offer audio wherever the text is, and say whose voice it is: a named narrator, or a clearly labelled synthetic voice. When the screen is on, let the text follow the voice at verse or sentence level, with pause and rewind. Build a full player that works with the screen off, with skip, speed and a sleep timer that stops at the end of a passage. In produced devotionals, name who wrote the reflections, keep Scripture distinct from commentary, make music optional, and end with silence or a choice rather than the next track.

What tends to go wrong

Unlabelled synthetic voices; voices that imitate a known pastor without consent; autoplay that runs into the next chapter or an advert; read-along that cannot be paused; music that cannot be turned off; reflections that sound like Scripture; audio that stops when the screen locks.

How to test it

Ask listeners whether a voice is human or synthetic and how they know. Listen on a commute with the screen off and check skip, pause and sleep. Compare recall of a passage heard with and without music. Test read-along with screen readers and people with dyslexia.

Related decision: The player works without the screen

Support listening as a complete mode of use.

Guidance

Make current passage, playback, interruption and resumption available through suitable device and accessibility controls. Offer speed and sleep options where supported. Preserve the last listening position and a route into reading.

What tends to go wrong

Voice-only stopping; a hidden player; resuming at the wrong chapter; treating listening as microphone consent.

How to test it

Test lock-screen use, interruptions, headphones, connectivity loss and switching to text. Confirm what the selected edition actually supports.

Related decision: Playback and reading positions can differ

Follow the narration without forcing the reader to follow every movement.

Guidance

Show the current playback anchor separately from the viewport. Let users follow or explore freely, then deliberately return to the spoken passage. Use continuous text where the task is sustained reading.

What tends to go wrong

Automatic recentering after manual scroll; stopping playback whenever someone explores; treating the visible paragraph as the actual playback position.

How to test it

Scroll away during playback, change text size and return to the spoken verse. Does each position remain understandable?

Related decision: Choose a devotional format

Compare a continuous reading, a sequence of cards and a chaptered audio session.

Guidance

Separate the content model from its presentation: Scripture, reflection, video, prayer and silence retain their identity in each format. A continuous page supports scanning; multi-page steps offer explicit pacing; story-style cards can combine short media segments. Offer visible Previous, Next and an outline rather than relying on swipes. Avoid timed advance for text by default. Audio needs pause, chapter navigation, transcript and an explicit end. Switching formats should preserve a semantic section where correspondence exists; do not invent word-level synchronisation.

What tends to go wrong

A story that advances before the reader finishes; tiny text fitted to one card; essential teaching available only in uncaptioned video; media playback counted as prayer; a disabled Next button until personal disclosure; automatic playback of another devotional.

How to test it

Move through a mixed-media session with sound off, large text, keyboard or screen reader navigation, and an unavailable video. Leave during a section and return. Verify the selected content version and section are restored, and ask what is required versus optional.

Faith and interpretation

“Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of Christ” (Romans 10:17). Scripture was written to be read aloud: “Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear” (Revelation 1:3). A voice is not neutral: tone, pace and emphasis interpret, as a reader in church does. Whose voice reads Scripture, and whether it is a person at all, is a question of trust as well as design.
