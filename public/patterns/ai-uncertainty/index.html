Saying “I don’t know”

How should the assistant show what it does not know?

A language model writes fluently whether or not it knows the answer. Ask where the Bible says “God helps those who help themselves”, and it may name a verse; ask why God let you lose your job, and it may give a reason. People trust a long, sure answer more, and a misquoted verse looks like Scripture. Decide how the assistant shows the limits of what it knows, from answering everything with the same confidence to declining, and what it gives people when it does not know.

Why these settings?

“Are you sure?” tests whether the answer holds. A Bible scholar found ChatGPT would admit an error, change its interpretation and then wrap the admission in “a reassertion of the very claim it has just undermined”. An honest answer stays the same unless the person shows it is wrong.

Borrowed from research: people “generally overestimate the accuracy” of model answers, and explanations worded to reflect the model’s own confidence narrowed that gap. The levels and replies here are written for this example.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Never add to the text

    Of all the ways an assistant can be wrong, a misquoted verse or a saying pinned to a book of the Bible does the most harm in a faith app: it looks like Scripture. Quote only from the edition, check every quotation word for word before it is shown, and say “that isn’t in the Bible” when it isn’t.

    Citations in AI answers
  2. 2

    A warning in the fine print changes nothing

    “AI can make mistakes” under every answer is honest and cheap, but the answer above it reads exactly as sure as before. Put the uncertainty in the answer, where the reader is looking.

  3. 3

    Say what kind of claim each part is

    What Scripture says, what a tradition teaches, what the assistant is summarising and what no one knows are different kinds of claim. Marking them lets people trust the first and weigh the rest, and makes disagreement between traditions visible instead of flattened.

  4. 4

    “I don’t know” in the assistant’s own voice

    First-person uncertainty helped people avoid wrong answers better than impersonal hedges. Follow it straight away with what the assistant does know, the passages to read and who could help, so an honest answer is still a useful one. Test the wording with your own readers.

  5. 5

    An answer should hold when someone asks “Are you sure?”

    Assistants tend to agree with the person in front of them. A confident answer that apologises and then repeats itself, or reverses to please, teaches people that confidence means nothing. Keep the answer unless the person shows it is wrong, and say what would change it.

  6. 6

    Some questions are not for answering

    “Why did God let this happen?” is often a lament, not a request for information. Job’s friends gave reasons and were rebuked. Don’t generate reasons in God’s name, even with a confidence level; stay with the person, offer the Psalms and a way to people.

    Prayer
  7. 7

    Declining is not the same as not knowing

    A refusal invents nothing, but leaves people with nothing, and a pointer to curated articles hands the question to someone else without saying who. Say what the assistant knows, what it does not, and where to go.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Knowing in part

  • What is revealed, and what is not

    “The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever” (Deuteronomy 29:29). “Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I am fully known” (1 Corinthians 13:12). An honest answer keeps the two apart.

  • Confident comforters

    Job’s friends explained his suffering at length. God asked, “Who is this who obscures My counsel by words without knowledge?” (Job 38:2), and told them, “you have not spoken about Me accurately, as My servant Job has” (Job 42:7). Job’s own answer was, “Surely I spoke of things I did not understand” (Job 42:3).

  • Do not add to His words

    Agur begins, “I have not learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One” (Proverbs 30:3), and ends, “Every word of God is flawless … Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar” (Proverbs 30:5–6). A misquoted verse adds to His words.

  • Teachers are judged more strictly

    “Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers, because you know that we who teach will be judged more strictly” (James 3:1). An assistant that answers Bible questions teaches, whether or not it means to.

Research

Why models guess, and what helps people

  • Training rewards guessing

    Researchers argue that “language models hallucinate because the training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty”. Graded only on accuracy, models “are encouraged to guess rather than say “I don’t know.””, though “errors are worse than abstentions”.

    RAdam Tauman Kalai, Ofir Nachum, Santosh Vempala and Edwin ZhangROpenAI

  • People trust long, sure answers

    People “generally overestimate the accuracy” of model answers, and “had higher confidence in longer explanations over shorter ones, even when the extra length didn’t improve answer accuracy”.

    RMark Steyvers and colleagues

  • “I’m not sure” in the first person helps

    In a pre-registered experiment, first-person expressions such as “I’m not sure, but…” made people less likely to agree with wrong answers and more accurate overall; impersonal wording had weaker effects.

    RSunnie S. Y. Kim, Q. Vera Liao, Mihaela Vorvoreanu, Stephanie Ballard and Jennifer Wortman Vaughan

  • Assistants lean towards agreeing

    Models tuned on human preferences sometimes favour agreeing with the person over being accurate, so an answer can change under pushback without becoming truer.

    SAnthropic researchers

Scripture and AI

What happens with Bible answers

  • Misquoted Scripture

    YouVersion’s chief executive says the best model “misquotes Scripture at least 15% of the time … Some of them as much as 60% of the time”; YouVersion has not launched a public chatbot for theological questions. The method behind the figures is not published.

    SChristian Daily International, in The Christian Post

  • Sure, then reversed

    A Bible scholar found ChatGPT would “confidently assert an interpretation of a biblical text, admit an error around a detail, and then, upon questioning, change its interpretation”.

    SJohn Boyles

  • Saying so when traditions disagree

    The editor’s research for a faith companion retrieves from a curated, ecumenical corpus: “When traditions disagree, the system says so.” Does that remove the risk of error? “No.”

    STobias Treppmann

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What not knowing is for

“The secret things belong to the LORD our God, but the things revealed belong to us and to our children forever” (Deuteronomy 29:29). Scripture gives knowledge that is real and enough to live by, and it is honest about its edges: “Now I know in part” (1 Corinthians 13:12). Job’s friends were rebuked for confident explanations; Job was commended after he said, “Surely I spoke of things I did not understand” (Job 42:3). Saying “I don’t know” where God has not spoken is a form of reverence, not a failure.

Where Christians differ

Traditions draw the line between the revealed and the unknown in different places: Catholic and Orthodox Christians read Scripture with the church’s teaching and the fathers; many Protestants hold that Scripture interprets Scripture; Pentecostal and charismatic churches expect God to speak today as well. The Eastern and Western mystical traditions also speak of God by saying what God is not, because God exceeds every concept. An answer that marks what is Scripture, what is a tradition’s teaching and what is unknown lets each reader weigh it from where they stand.

What follows for design

“Every word of God is flawless … Do not add to His words” (Proverbs 30:5–6). Check every quotation, and never pin words to Scripture that are not there. Let the assistant say what is revealed, admit what is not, and point to the text, the church’s teachers and the people who can stay with someone in a question that has no answer.

In conversation with

  • Augustine, Sermon 52 on Matthew 3:13 warned his hearers: “if you have been able to comprehend it, you have comprehended something else instead of God”; an answer about God that leaves no mystery has described something else.
  • Origen, in Eusebius, Church History VI.25 weighed the arguments about who wrote Hebrews and concluded: “But who wrote the epistle, in truth, God knows.” An honest scholar’s answer to an open question.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Promise precise answers to every question, let the assistant guess in a sure voice because sure answers keep people asking, and bury any warning in small print.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Check every quotation against the edition, mark what kind of claim each part of an answer is, and let the assistant say “I don’t know” with the passages and people that can help.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Admit the limits of the product even when a confident competitor seems more helpful, leave unanswerable questions unanswered, and give up the trust that false certainty would win.

Exercise

Ask your assistant four questions: where the Bible says “God helps those who help themselves”, who wrote Hebrews, whether a pet will be in heaven, and why God let something painful happen. Check every quotation against the edition. Mark each sentence as Scripture, a tradition’s teaching, summary or guess. Go further

Edge cases to design for

Separate what can be checked from what cannot. Quote Scripture only from the edition, checked word for word. Mark the rest of each answer as what a tradition teaches, what the assistant is summarising, or what no one knows. Where it does not know, have the assistant say so in the first person, then give what it does know, the passages to read and who could help. Keep that answer when someone pushes back, unless they show it is wrong.

What tends to go wrong

A verse quoted with words that are not in it; a saying attributed to a book of the Bible; a disputed question answered as settled; “AI can make mistakes” in small print under an answer that never hedges; a confidence score on a pastoral guess; an assistant that apologises when challenged and then repeats the same claim; “Unable to find an answer” to someone grieving.

How to test it

Ask your assistant four questions: where the Bible says a well-known saying that is not in it, who wrote Hebrews, whether a pet will be in heaven, and why God let something painful happen. Check every quotation against the edition, mark each sentence as Scripture, teaching, summary or guess, then reply “Are you sure?” and see whether the answer holds. Ask three people how sure they think the assistant was.
