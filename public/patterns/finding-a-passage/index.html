Finding a passage

Browse, type a reference, or start from a need?

Someone hears “John 3” in a sermon. Someone else wants Isaiah but isn’t sure where it sits. A third person only wants today’s reading. One picker rarely serves all three, and sometimes the best picker is none at all. Opening the right page for someone is a small hospitality, and the first step of every reading.

Why these settings?

These switches are separate decisions from the options. Opening on tap is faster; reviewing first helps when a mis-tap is costly or the destination is unfamiliar. A book grid fits all 66 books in one view, with no scrolling, at the cost of recognisability: it shows three-character codes (here the USFM identifiers), which suit people who already know the books. Familiar convention

1. Step by step

A widely used structure, generalized here. Not a copy of a particular app.

Try this

Tap “Mark 3”, choose John, then chapter 3. Then switch on the book grid and the verse step and try again.

+ Makes easier

Each screen asks one question, with large targets. Readers who recognise a book name but do not remember a reference can always get there.

− Costs

Several screens for a destination the person already knows. Sixty-six books make a long list; a grid is shorter but relies on abbreviations.

Ask your teamDo most of your readers know where they are going, or are they finding out?

Observed

2. Expandable book list

Observed in Mere Bible and YouVersion: chapters open inside the book list (see the screenshots below). Stills do not show gesture or commit timing, so opening follows the switches below, as in option 1.

Try this

Open the picker. The list opens at Mark with chapter 3 marked. Scroll to John, expand it and choose 3.

+ Makes easier

Book context stays visible while choosing a chapter, and the reader’s current book opens ready.

− Costs

Long books push the rest of the list down. Psalms puts 150 cells inside the list, and the scroll position has to survive a book collapsing.

Ask your teamWill your longest books still be manageable inside a list?

Borrowed

3. Type a reference

Borrowed from address bars and command palettes, where people type a known destination. In a Bible app, the parser decides what counts as a reference.

Try this

Type “jo”, then “jo 3”, then “jn 3:16”. Press Enter to open.

+ Makes easier

The quickest route to a known reference, such as a sermon slide or a citation in a book.

− Costs

It assumes the person knows the reference and can spell it. Ambiguous abbreviations and mistakes need clear help. It looks up references only; it does not search words or use AI.

Ask your teamWhat should happen when someone types something that is not a reference?

Observed

4. Start from a feeling

Observed in ESV (“When I’m Feeling” audio playlists) and Glorify (search for “chapters, verses or emotions” with chips such as Anxiety and Doubt); see the screenshots below. The passages in this example are our own illustrative choice.

Try this

Choose “Weary”, then open Matthew 11:28–30. Then go back and look at what the lists leave out.

+ Makes easier

Someone who does not know where to look can still find a way in, starting from where they are rather than from a reference.

− Costs

Someone else has decided which verses answer which feeling. Short lists can turn Scripture into remedies, lift verses out of their context and imply how the reader ought to feel. Acute distress needs more than a verse.

Ask your teamWho chooses the passages for each feeling, and how will readers know?

Leave it out

5. No passage picker

An exploratory proposal for plan- or session-focused readers. Not attributed to a specific app and untested.

Try this

Try to reach John 3. Then use the back arrow to see what the plan offers.

+ Makes easier

Nothing to decide before reading. The plan supplies the passage and the back arrow returns to the plan, which can suit a guided session, a devotional or a group reading.

− Costs

John 3 is out of reach. Someone who wants to follow a reference, check context or wander has to leave for another app or mode.

Ask your teamIs open-ended lookup part of this product’s job, or does it belong somewhere else?

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    It depends on what the reader already knows

    Typing is fastest when the reference is in front of someone, and a wall when it isn’t. Browsing takes more taps but never asks anyone to remember. The expandable list sits between the two: the shape of the book stays in view while you choose.

  2. 2

    Leaving it out is a real option

    If your product guides people through a plan, a full picker is an exit as much as a feature. Leaving it out keeps the session calm and the scope honest. It also sends someone following a sermon to another app.

  3. 3

    A feeling asks a different question

    Not “where is it?” but “where should I begin?”. ESV and Glorify both offer it. It welcomes someone with no reference in mind, and hands quiet authority to whoever wrote the lists. Name the curators, open passages in their chapter, and keep acute distress out of a verse menu.

  4. 4

    Open on tap; review only when it costs something

    Opening on tap is what most people expect. A review step earns its place when a wrong destination is costly, such as changing a group’s shared reading. Going down to the verse helps someone following a sermon and slows down someone reading whole chapters.

  5. 5

    Offer both routes, and keep each one plain

    Most general Bible readers end up with browsing and typed entry. That works while each route says what it does. A reference field that quietly becomes text search or an AI answer box changes what people expect from it.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Finding the place

  • Finding the place is an old practice

    In Nazareth, “the scroll of the prophet Isaiah was handed to Him. Unrolling it, He found the place where it was written” (Luke 4:17). Before chapters and verses, passages were named by what happened in them: “have you not read about the burning bush in the Book of Moses?” (Mark 12:26). Chapters date from the thirteenth century and verse numbers from 1551.

  • Easy access is a stated commitment

    The Second Vatican Council asked that “easy access to Sacred Scripture should be provided for all the Christian faithful”. Protestant Bible societies have pursued the same goal through translation and distribution. A picker is where that access succeeds or fails on a phone.

    TSecond Vatican Council

How people arrive

Lookup, suggestion and habit

  • People reach Scripture in several ways

    Lifeway’s 2024 survey finds looking up passages, reading ones suggested by others, rereading favourites and reading systematically overlap in the same people. One route will not serve all of them.

    SLifeway Research

  • Apps teach a way of reading

    Hutchings shows Bible apps are designed as persuasive technologies that train reading habits. By the same logic, a picker that always opens at a verse teaches verse reading, and one that opens at a chapter teaches passages.

    RTim Hutchings

  • Reading on needs no picker

    YouVersion documents continuous chapter scrolling and an optional verse picker: for people reading on, the next chapter matters more than a destination.

    SYouVersion Support

Interface research

What helps people find

  • Recognition is easier than recall

    Interfaces that show the options ask less of memory than fields that need someone to remember and type. Browsing books and chapters asks people to recognise; a reference field asks them to recall. Offer both.

    DRaluca Budiu

  • Forgive the way people type

    When input is wrong and a correction is known, suggest it. Accept abbreviations, other languages’ book names and “Psalm” for “Psalms”, and say “Did you mean John 3?” instead of “Not found”.

    AW3CDUSFM documentation

  • Many small targets

    A grid of 150 Psalms or 176 verses packs many targets into a phone screen. Keep each at least 24 pixels with space between, and let the grid reflow when text is enlarged.

    AW3CDW3C WAI

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What finding the place is for

In Nazareth, Jesus unrolled the scroll and “found the place where it was written” (Luke 4:17). Knowing where a passage sits, in which book and among which neighbours, is part of understanding it. Finding the place is a small act of attention before reading.

Where Christians differ

The Bible itself is not the same book everywhere. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include books that most Protestant Bibles omit or place in a separate Apocrypha, and the Hebrew Bible orders its books differently from Christian Old Testaments. Many readers know no references at all and come with a feeling or a story.

What follows for design

Use the canon and book order of the edition being read, and do not impose one tradition’s list on everyone. Serve the reader who knows no references as carefully as the expert, without steering either toward what the publisher wants them to read.

In conversation with

  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) “Easy access to Sacred Scripture should be provided for all the Christian faithful”: finding the passage is the first step of access.
  • Martin Luther, Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen (1530) designed his Bible for ordinary people; a picker should serve the reader who knows no references as well as the scholar.

Edge cases to design for

Offer book browsing and direct reference entry as complementary routes. Preserve the current location, make back and cancel predictable, and show the full destination before opening a parsed reference.

What tends to go wrong

Unexplained abbreviations; assuming every edition has the same book list; resetting book selection on back; accepting an ambiguous reference without clarification.

How to test it

Locate an unfamiliar book, enter an abbreviated range, cancel after choosing a chapter and return to the original verse. Test long book names, different canons, large text and one-chapter books.

How the example is built, and what it leaves out

Built with React, Tailwind and an adapted shadcn/ui Button, styled with the paper visual vocabulary used across these examples. Input stays in browser memory. The examples do not request an AI response, load more than a few verses of Bible text or save reading history.

Escape cancels. Opening or cancelling returns focus to the reader’s passage button, and each new screen moves focus to its heading. Chapter cells are ordinary toggle buttons, not an ARIA grid. The comparison selector follows the ARIA tabs pattern.

A production implementation still needs a licensed content catalogue, edition-specific book and chapter availability, loading and error states, and testing with assistive technology. The visual style is illustrative, not a required brand treatment.

Other approaches are not shown here: separate or combined translation, book and chapter controls; a two-pane browser on wider screens; verse and range selection; Mere Bible’s chapter rail beside the text; a single field that combines book filtering, references and AI suggestions; and different canons or book orders.

Related decision: A reference opens at a deliberate destination

Choose the content scope, landing position and temporary cue separately.

Guidance

For contextual reading, open the containing chapter and place the first requested verse in view. For a focused preview, show only the verse or requested passage with a Read in context action. A chapter overview may open at the top, but retain the requested reference and provide Jump to verse. Scrolling and highlighting are independent: landing at a verse can use a temporary outline, a labelled margin cue, or no highlight. Mark a requested range rather than only its first verse when helpful. Temporary navigation cues never become saved annotations. Account for headers, players and delayed layout; retain back navigation to the search or chat.

What tends to go wrong

Opening at the chapter top without acknowledging the requested verse; permanently marking a deep-link target; hiding a focused excerpt’s boundaries; losing the origin.

How to test it

Enter one verse, a range and a cross-chapter passage from search, chat and an external link. Check large text, cold loading, missing content and return navigation.

Related decision: Book and chapter pickers: list, grid or drill-down?

Choose the browsing shape independently from the search field.

Guidance

Start with full book names and a clear selected state. Compare a list, a compact grid and progressive book-to-chapter selection for the intended readers. Keep search available, preserve the current location on cancel, and use the selected edition’s actual book and chapter availability.

What tends to go wrong

Unexplained abbreviations; tiny grid targets; assuming one canon or ordering; losing the list position when returning from a chapter; an unavailable book silently opening elsewhere.

How to test it

Find an unfamiliar book, select a chapter, go back and cancel. Repeat with long translated names, enlarged text, a one-chapter book and missing edition coverage.

Related decision: How do readers move to the next chapter?

Compare continuous reading, explicit buttons and a persistent navigation bar.

Guidance

Match navigation to the current task, not a permanent reader category. Continuous reading can cross a visible chapter boundary without permanent arrows. Discrete chapters benefit from explicit previous and next destinations. A compact bar suits frequent movement, while study may additionally need a back trail, saved place and direct reference entry. A bounded passage can end without a prominent Next action; retain optional wider context where useful. Keep controls clear of the final lines, audio controls and selection tools.

What tends to go wrong

Swipe-only navigation; wrapping from the last book to the first without warning; hiding the last verse behind a bar; changing chapter during text selection; a missing download leaving a blank page.

How to test it

Give participants different tasks: continue a narrative, compare distant references, follow a spoken reference, resume a note and finish one shared passage. Observe misnavigation, return to place and unwanted interruptions. Ask whether they wanted a next step; do not infer usefulness from the number of chapters opened. Include large text, assistive technology and unavailable chapters.

Related decision: One field for browsing, references and AI discovery

Let one input filter books, recognise a reference and suggest passages, while making each result’s origin clear.

Guidance

Keep one field above the book browser. As the person types, filter book names immediately and recognise complete references locally. Other queries can request AI-assisted passage suggestions after a pause, with book matches and suggestions visible together. Prioritise a resolved reference, preserve the query and make opening a deliberate action. Clearly label AI suggestions and any exact text matches as different kinds of result. A separate full-text search service or sourced answer preview is an additional capability, not implied by a unified field.

What tends to go wrong

Presenting type-ahead filtering, direct references and AI suggestions as separate mandatory modes; sending unfinished references without a clear policy; allowing late suggestions to replace current results; describing AI suggestions as exact wording matches; jumping before the person finishes a range.

How to test it

Start empty, type a partial book name, extend it into a reference and then a range, and replace it with a topic. Check coexisting result groups, loading, network failure, no matches, stale responses, keyboard focus, editing before opening and returning from a chapter grid. Inspect what text is sent remotely and when.
