How much text at once

One word, a verse, a passage or a whole chapter?

People increasingly read Scripture on phones, in short and frequent sessions. Apps answer with verse cards, focus modes, read-along highlighting and even speed readers. Each decides how much of the Bible someone holds in mind at once, from a single word to a whole chapter. The church has read in many measures. An app should help people hear the word, not just get through it.

Why these settings?

Verse numbers are a sixteenth-century addition: helpful for finding and citing, and a reason people read sentences apart from their argument. Hiding them gives a reader’s-edition feel. Read-aloud is simulated: nothing plays, but the focus or highlight moves at about 180 words a minute, as it would with narration. Checking off verses is a different question from how much text is shown: it decides what counts as read. Observed in Dewfall, a small iPhone app that tracks verses read, it turns each verse into its own row with a box to tick, whatever the unit. Here only the box marks a verse; opening or scrolling past one does not. One word at a time has its own speed control and ignores all three settings.

Borrowed

1. One word at a time

Borrowed from speed-reading tools such as Spritz and Kindle Word Runner, which flash one word at a time at a chosen speed.

Try this

Press Start at 250 words a minute. Halfway through, try to look back at what the disciples said.

+ Makes easier

No eye movement and no lost place; it can feel fast and very focused for short, familiar text.

− Costs

You cannot glance back, and readers reread 10–15% of the time; comprehension tends to drop. It also turns Scripture into a speed.

Ask your teamIs there any reading in your product that should be faster rather than slower?

Observed

2. One verse at a time

Observed in Glorify, which steps through a short passage one verse per screen with a segmented bar (see the screenshots below).

Try this

Step through to the end of the passage. Then switch on Context and see the verses either side.

+ Makes easier

An easy start and a slow pace that suits meditation and memory. Each verse gets the whole screen.

− Costs

The chapter is out of sight, and a lone verse is easy to read or share apart from its setting.

Ask your teamHow far is every verse from its context, and how does someone get there?

Observed

3. One verse in focus

Observed in Dwell and Soultime during listening (current verse bright, the rest dimmed or hidden); borrowed from iA Writer’s focus mode and karaoke captions.

Try this

Move through with Next verse, then tap a verse further up. Switch on read-aloud and press play.

+ Makes easier

Attention on one verse with the passage still in view; with audio it keeps reader and narrator together.

− Costs

Dimmed text must stay readable and reachable for screen readers, and moving text needs a pause.

Ask your teamWill the focus follow the reader, the narrator, or both?

Observed

4. One passage

Observed in Glorify’s passage readings (Judges 6:11–16 with its own audio); lectionaries, plans and sermons also assign passages. Titles here are our own.

Try this

Read the passage, then open “Before this” to see the parable that comes first.

+ Makes easier

A clear beginning and end, the unit sermons and study guides use, and the neighbouring passages one tap away.

− Costs

Where a passage begins and ends, and what it is called, are editorial decisions. Some readers will want the whole chapter.

Ask your teamWho sets the passage boundaries and titles, and how will readers know?

Familiar convention

5. Whole chapter

A familiar convention in Bible apps and e-books; reader’s editions such as the ESV Reader’s Bible remove numbers and headings for longer reading.

Try this

Scroll up from where you stopped to the sower, then switch verse numbers off for a reader’s edition.

+ Makes easier

The argument and story stay whole; the best fit for reading a book through.

− Costs

Long on a phone, and one verse is harder to hold in mind. Headings and numbers shape the reading as much as they help.

Ask your teamDo your readers mostly read through, or dip in?

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Phones invite shorter, more selective reading

    Screen reading involves more scanning, keyword spotting and selective reading, and less sustained reading (Liu). Comprehension on screens falls behind paper mainly under time pressure, not when people read at their own pace (Delgado et al.). Dyer found app readers completed more days of a plan, while men understood less than in print. Smaller units fit the sessions; they do not guarantee understanding.

  2. 2

    One word at a time removes looking back

    Readers look back at earlier words 10–15% of the time, and taking that away tends to reduce comprehension (Rayner et al.). For Scripture, which rewards rereading, it is the weakest fit; it also invites reading as a speed. If you offer it, keep it for short, familiar text, with a rewind.

  3. 3

    A smaller unit has a long devotional history

    Lectio divina reads a few verses slowly and repeatedly. A verse at a time, or a verse in focus, can support that kind of attention and memorisation, as long as the passage is one tap away. Verse numbers date from 1551; a verse was never meant to stand alone. How a reader moves from one card to the next, in order or in a feed that never ends, is a separate decision.

    Feeds, cards and stories
  4. 4

    Focus with context is the middle path

    A highlighted verse with the passage dimmed around it keeps attention and context together, and works best with audio: read-along highlighting helped children with dyslexia follow, and karaoke-style subtitles have supported literacy at scale. Let readers choose the window, as Immersive Reader does with one, three or five lines.

  5. 5

    Checking off verses is a second dimension

    Some apps let readers tick off each verse as read; Dewfall, a small iPhone app, does this and counts verses read toward finishing the Bible. That is not another point between one verse and a whole chapter. It decides what counts as read, and it works at any unit. Like one verse per card, it makes the verse the unit, splitting a passage into separate rows, but the passage stays in view. A tick can help someone keep their own place in a long book. It can also reward getting through over taking in: Dyer found app readers finished more of a plan without understanding more. If you offer it, make the tick a deliberate act, never infer it from opening or scrolling, and let it be undone.

  6. 6

    Passage and chapter carry the argument

    A passage gives a reading its natural shape, the unit preachers and plans use, but its boundaries and titles are editorial. Reader’s editions such as the ESV Reader’s Bible remove numbers and headings so people read longer and follow the story. Make the longer mode calm and easy to enter, and keep the reader’s place across modes.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

How the church has read

  • Read aloud, together, and whole

    Israel heard the law read publicly: “they read from the Book of the Law of God, explaining it and giving insight” (Nehemiah 8:8). Paul’s letters were read aloud to whole congregations, “after this letter has been read among you” (Colossians 4:16), and Timothy is told, “devote yourself to the public reading of Scripture” (1 Timothy 4:13). For most of Christian history, Scripture was heard in continuous portions.

  • Silent, private reading came later

    Augustine was struck that Ambrose read with his eyes on the page and his voice silent. Personal reading spread with literacy, printing and vernacular Bibles. A phone continues that private line, and can also reconnect people with reading aloud and together.

    HTranslation and commentary at Sententiae Antiquae

  • Small units for prayer and memory

    “I have hidden Your word in my heart” (Psalm 119:11). Lectio divina dwells on a few verses read slowly and repeatedly; memorisation works verse by verse. Small units are not a modern shortcut; they have their own devotional history.

    TUniversity of St Andrews, School of Divinity

  • Context guards meaning

    Chapter divisions date from the thirteenth century and verse numbers from 1551. Fee and Stuart’s principle, that a text cannot mean what it could never have meant for its first readers, depends on reading a verse within its passage, book and genre.

    TZondervan, 4th edition 2014 (quotations listing)

Digital reading today

What people actually do

  • Most Bible reading is now on screens

    Two-thirds of US Bible users access the Bible digitally. YouVersion reports over a billion installs and named Isaiah 41:10 its most engaged-with verse of 2025, the fourth time in six years: much digital engagement happens one verse at a time.

    RAmerican Bible SocietyDChurchLeaders, reporting YouVersion

  • Screen reading is more selective

    People report more browsing, scanning, keyword spotting and one-time reading on screens, and less concentrated reading (Liu). Hutchings shows Bible apps are designed to train particular reading habits.

    RZiming LiuRTim Hutchings

  • Completion is not comprehension

    In Dyer’s church study, app readers finished about two more days of a ten-day plan than print readers, while men understood less on screen. A unit that is easy to finish may not be the one that is understood.

    RJohn Dyer

Reading science

Different readings, different needs

  • Normal reading is about 240 words a minute

    Adults read non-fiction at about 238 words a minute, most between 175 and 300. The storm on the sea takes about a minute to read: short enough for any morning.

    RMarc Brysbaert

  • Skimming finds, deep reading understands

    Skimmers jump ahead once a section stops paying off; they catch the main points but understand less. Speed-reading displays remove the looking back that comprehension relies on.

    RDuggan and PayneRRayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman

  • Listening is reading too

    One study found no difference in comprehension between reading, listening, and doing both. Read-along modes serve people who listen as much as people who read.

    RRogowsky, Calhoun and TallalRInternational Journal of Emerging Technologies in Learning

  • Memory grows by recall

    Students who practised recalling a passage remembered 61% a week later; those who reread it remembered 40%. A verse-at-a-time mode for memorisation should ask, not just show.

    RRoediger and Karpicke

  • Pace matters more than medium

    Screens fall behind paper mainly when people read under time pressure. Let readers set the pace.

    RDelgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón

Accessible reading

Best practice

  • Anything that moves needs a pause

    Content that moves or updates for more than five seconds needs a way to pause, stop or hide it: auto-advancing verses, read-along and word-by-word display all qualify.

    AW3C

  • Measure, spacing and size

    Aim for lines under 80 characters, line spacing of at least 1.5, unjustified text and resizing to 200% without sideways scrolling.

    AW3C

  • Let readers choose the window

    Immersive Reader lets people focus on one, three or five lines. Offer the size of the focus as a setting, and respect reduced motion.

    DMicrosoft

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What attention is for

Mary “sat at the Lord’s feet listening to His message” (Luke 10:39), and Jesus said she had chosen the good part. Lectio divina dwells on a few verses slowly; reading a whole letter at once shows its argument. Both are forms of attention, and attention is a form of love.

Where Christians differ

Devotional traditions favour short passages for meditation and memory. Biblical scholars and many teachers urge reading whole books, warning that verse numbers invite reading sentences apart from their context. Silent private reading, which Augustine found remarkable in Ambrose, is itself a later development beside public reading aloud.

What follows for design

The question is not which unit is more spiritual, but whether the screen keeps a verse connected to its passage and leaves the reader’s attention free. Design for the reader to finish, put the phone down and live what they read (James 1:22).

In conversation with

  • Albert Borgmann, Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life (1984) contrasted “focal practices” that ask for attention and skill with devices that deliver commodities effortlessly; reading Scripture is a focal practice a device can support or displace.
  • Eugene Peterson, Eat This Book (2006) calls for slow, attentive reading in which the text is taken in, not skimmed.

Edge cases to design for

Decide which reading this screen serves. For meditation or memory, show a verse at a time or a verse in focus with the passage dimmed around it. For listening, move the focus with the audio. For a daily reading, show one passage with its reference, what comes before and after, and a way into the chapter. For a book read through, offer calm long-form text. Keep the same place when someone changes mode, and never strand a verse without a route to its context.

What tends to go wrong

A verse shown alone with no way to its chapter; a box to tick on every verse that turns a passage into a to-do list, or ticks verses merely opened; editorial headings presented as Scripture; word-by-word display used for study; auto-advancing text with no pause or rewind; focus modes that hide the text from screen readers; losing the reader’s place when switching modes.

How to test it

Ask people to read the same passage in two modes, then retell it and answer a question that depends on the verse before. Time how long it takes to find the context of a single verse. Test focus and read-along with screen readers, reduced motion and people with dyslexia or attention difficulties.

Related decision: A bounded passage with a route to wider context

Choose the reading unit by the task while keeping its boundaries and context visible.

Guidance

Present the full chosen passage, its reference and edition. Explain editorial headings and passage boundaries. Offer preceding and following context and continuous reading without losing the original selection.

What tends to go wrong

Clipping a passage without notice; treating one boundary as universal across editions; replacing source text with an AI summary; making context available only through chat.

How to test it

Compare focused and continuous layouts for orientation, comprehension, voluntary rereading and context access. Include poetry and passages crossing chapter boundaries.

Faith and interpretation

Verse numbers date from the sixteenth century and chapter divisions from the thirteenth; neither is part of the inspired text, and both make it easy to read a sentence apart from its argument. Smaller units also have a long devotional history: lectio divina dwells on a few verses read slowly and repeatedly. The question is not which unit is more spiritual, but whether the screen keeps the verse connected to the passage it belongs to.
