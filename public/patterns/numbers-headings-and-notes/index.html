Numbers, headings and notes

How much should surround the text?

Almost nothing around the words of the Bible is part of them. Verse numbers, chapter breaks, section titles, red letters, footnotes, study notes and now teaching videos were added by printers, translators and editors over centuries. Each helps someone find, follow or understand, and each quietly teaches them how to read. Whoever adds to the page today joins a long line of printers and editors, and shares their responsibility.

Why these settings?

Verse per line was the norm in printed Bibles for centuries and is still common in apps; paragraphs follow the sense of the text. Red letters were introduced in 1899 by Louis Klopsch. Both are editorial choices that sit alongside the six options, and in “Reader decides” they can also be changed in the drawer.

Familiar convention

1. Reader’s edition

Familiar from print reader’s editions such as the ESV Reader’s Bible and Biblica’s The Books of the Bible, which remove numbers, headings and notes.

Try this

Read from the mustard seed to the end of the storm. Then try to tell someone where “Be still!” is.

+ Makes easier

The text reads as continuous writing; nothing interrupts, and nothing suggests reading sentence by sentence.

− Costs

Harder to find or cite a verse, or to follow a preacher who says “verse 39”.

What we noticed

Takeaways

  1. Everything around the text is someone’s addition

    Chapters date from the thirteenth century, verse numbers from 1551, red letters from 1899. Section titles and notes come from translators and editors. Genette calls this a threshold of interpretation: it shapes how the text is received before anyone reads a word.

  2. Notes have always carried theology

    The Geneva Bible’s marginal notes were partisan enough that the 1604 rules for the King James Version banned notes except for Hebrew and Greek words. Three centuries later, the Scofield notes carried dispensationalism to millions. A study layout is a voice beside the text, and readers should know whose.

  3. Headings help structure, and steer meaning

    Headings improve memory for how a text is organised, and titles bias comprehension towards what they name. “The storm on the sea” and “Jesus calms the storm” point a reader at different things. Style titles so they cannot be mistaken for Scripture, and say who wrote them.

  4. Every marker is a decision

    In hypertext research, links add decisions and visual processing that can impair reading, most for less experienced readers. Keep markers few and typed, open notes in a sheet over the passage, and return to the same verse.

  5. Media inside the text is a voice and a cost

    The Joseph Prince app pins video, image, audio and book-excerpt cards to exact verses, some locked for subscribers, and hides the layer in paragraph mode. Teaching at the verse can help, but learning research finds vivid, non-essential extras reduce recall and understanding. Say whose teaching it is, keep it switchable, and think hard before putting a paywall inside Scripture.

  6. Defaults decide for most people

    Mere Bible lets readers switch numbers, headings, note markers and red letters. That serves people who look for it; most never change a default. Choose the default for your main reading, and make the switches easy to find.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

History and tradition

How the apparatus arrived

  • Numbers and chapters are late arrivals

    Stephen Langton’s chapters date from the thirteenth century; Robert Estienne numbered New Testament verses in 1551, and the Geneva Bible of 1560 was the first English Bible to use them.

    HBrewminate

  • Notes were contested from the start

    The 1604 rules for the King James translators allowed no marginal notes except for Hebrew or Greek words, partly in reaction to the Geneva notes. The Scofield Reference Bible of 1909 shows the opposite: notes that taught a framework to millions.

    HChristian History InstituteHMuseum of the Bible

  • Red letters and reader’s editions

    Red letters began with Louis Klopsch in 1899. Reader’s editions, such as Biblica’s The Books of the Bible and the ESV Reader’s Bible, remove numbers, titles and notes so the books can be read whole, and churches have read them together.

    HCrosswaySScripture Engagement (Taylor University)DCrossway

Interpretation

Why apparatus is never neutral

  • A threshold, not a border

    Genette describes titles and notes as a threshold that influences how a text is received. For Scripture, that means headings and notes are interpretation, however modest.

    SGérard Genette

  • Context guards meaning

    Reading a verse within its passage and genre is the first rule of interpretation in widely used evangelical guides. Numbers make it easy to lift a verse out; headings and paragraphs keep the passage visible.

    TZondervan, 4th edition 2014 (quotations listing)

Reading science

What helps and what costs

  • Headings help memory for structure

    Signals such as headings improve memory for a text’s organisation, and titles bias comprehension towards their topic.

    RLorch and colleagues

  • Extras can crowd out the essential

    In a meta-analysis of learning studies, interesting but non-essential details, especially visual ones, reduced both recall and transfer.

    RThe eLearning Coach, summarising Rey (2012) and Mayer’s coherence principle

  • Markers cost attention

    Links and markers add decisions that can impair reading, especially for readers with less prior knowledge or working memory; clearly typed and restricted links help.

    RDeStefano and LeFevre

Accessible reading

Best practice

  • Size, spacing and emphasis

    Aim for 16–19 px text, 1.5 line spacing and left alignment, and mark additions with weight or colour rather than italics.

    ABritish Dyslexia Association, 2023AW3C

  • Notes that return to the place

    Open notes over the passage, move focus into the note and back to the marker when it closes, and keep markers large enough to tap without crowding the line.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What the page around Scripture is for

“Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar” (Proverbs 30:6). Chapter and verse numbers, headings and notes are all additions, made over centuries to help readers find and follow the text. They serve the words; they are not the words.

Where Christians differ

The King James translators banned interpretive notes; the Scofield Reference Bible carried a whole interpretive system in its notes; reader’s editions remove almost everything. Red letters for the words of Jesus are loved by some and questioned by others, since all Scripture is “God-breathed” (2 Timothy 3:16). No choice is neutral.

What follows for design

Make every addition look like an addition and name who added it. Default to the least apparatus your readers need, and let them add more. Never place promotion inside the text, where it borrows Scripture’s authority.

In conversation with

  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) asks for “suitable footnotes”: apparatus is welcomed, and judged by whether it suits the reader.
  • Augustine, On Christian Doctrine I.36.40 gives the test for any note: does it build up love of God and neighbour?

Edge cases to design for

Choose a default by the main reading the product serves: a reader’s edition for reading through, numbers and titles for finding and following, notes and study material for study. Make every addition look like an addition: lighter type for numbers, a different style for titles, markers that open notes in a sheet and return to the same verse. Say who wrote headings and notes. Offer a settings drawer that changes the apparatus without moving the reader’s place, and keep poetry lineation and paragraphing from the edition.

What tends to go wrong

Headings styled like Scripture; study notes that read as the text’s own voice; note markers too small to tap or that jump away from the passage; settings that reflow and lose the reader’s place; red letters or headings that cannot be switched off; verse-per-line layouts that break poetry and paragraphs.

How to test it

Ask readers which parts of the screen are Scripture and which were added. Open a note and return: does the reader land on the same verse? Switch settings mid-chapter and check the place is kept. Compare retelling after reading the same passage as a reader’s edition and as a study layout.

Earlier pattern: Verse numbers that help without taking over

Separate locating a verse from the precision needed to select it.

Guidance

Use modest inline numbers for a general reading default. Compare margin numbers for reference-heavy tasks and an optional quiet view. Preserve an accessible way to reveal references and select a verse without tapping a tiny superscript.

What tends to go wrong

Colour-only selection; hiding numbers with no way to recover a reference; a long-press-only action; copying text without its edition and reference.

How to test it

Find and select a verse with enlarged text, keyboard and screen reader. Check that hiding visual numbers does not remove reference access.

Earlier pattern: Paragraphs or one verse per line?

Compare paragraph reading with a separate line for each verse, while preserving the edition’s poetry and structure.

Guidance

Begin with the edition’s paragraph and poetry structure for continuous reading. Offer a verse-per-line preference when reference finding is central, and retain meaningful paragraph and poetry distinctions in either mode.

What tends to go wrong

Flattening poetry into prose; treating verse breaks as sentence boundaries; changing layout and losing the selected passage.

How to test it

Compare sustained reading and reference lookup at large text sizes. Ask readers to locate a verse, change layout and explain where the paragraph ends.

Earlier pattern: Editorial distinctions survive reflow

Preserve poetry, headings and translation additions without forcing a rigid layout.

Guidance

Reflow paragraphs and preserve relative poetic structure. Distinguish section headings from Scripture. Explain translator-supplied words when the edition marks them. Verse navigation can coexist with continuous prose.

What tends to go wrong

Shrinking text to preserve a desktop layout; treating each verse as an unrelated box; calling translation additions AI-generated text.

How to test it

Test narrow screens, enlarged fonts and spoken output. Can readers recognise a heading, poetic grouping and a translation addition without relying only on colour?

Earlier pattern: Footnotes without losing place

Inspect a note and return to the exact reading anchor.

Guidance

Attach the note to its text anchor and preserve its parts: origin reference, quoted phrase, explanatory text and alternative reading where supplied. Choose inline expansion, a mobile sheet or a study panel by note length and available space.

What tends to go wrong

Hover-only access; a modal with no usable close control; merging quoted wording and editorial explanation; closing to a different reading position.

How to test it

Test opening and closing with touch, keyboard and screen reader at large text sizes. Confirm focus and the visible reading position return to the note anchor.

Earlier pattern: Adjust reading settings without losing the passage

Keep typography, appearance and text-layer preferences close to the reading experience.

Guidance

Offer a labelled reading-settings entry near the passage. A bottom drawer keeps some context visible on mobile; a side panel can retain more readable context on wider screens. Preview typography and appearance against real text. Group font, size and spacing separately from optional verse numbers, notes and section headings. Preserve the semantic reading anchor after reflow. Explain whether changes apply immediately, provide reset, and distinguish persistent preferences from a temporary view.

What tends to go wrong

Tiny slider-only adjustments; unlabeled spacing icons; hiding verse numbers and losing reference access; hiding a note marker as though the note were deleted; fixed drawer height that clips controls at large text; changing edition while claiming to change appearance.

How to test it

Open settings midway through a long chapter, increase text size, change spacing and hide verse numbers. Dismiss and locate the same verse. Repeat with keyboard, screen reader, narrow viewport, poetry and large system text. Test reset and returning after restart if persistence is promised.

Faith and interpretation

Traditions disagree about apparatus. King James forbade marginal notes because the Geneva notes argued politics and theology; the Scofield notes later carried a whole system of interpretation to millions of readers. Reader’s editions remove everything so the books can be read whole. None of these is neutral: what surrounds the text quietly teaches people how to read it.
