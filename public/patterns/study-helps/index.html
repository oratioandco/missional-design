Study helps

Where should commentary and notes open?

Commentaries, cross-references, study notes and sermon clips can answer a question at the verse where it arises, or pull the reader away from the text for good. Where the help opens decides which. Decide how readers find help, where it opens, whose voice it carries, and how they get back to the verse. Help should serve the reader’s encounter with the text, never replace it.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. Keep the verse in sight, or bring the reader back to it

    People learn more when related material is placed together, and interruptions are paid for in stress and lost threads. A preview under the verse or a sheet that leaves it visible keeps the two together. When help takes the whole screen, Back should land on the same verse and show it.

  2. A panel must say what it is following

    Olive Tree’s Resource Guide follows the passage; a pinned panel stays on its verse. Both are useful, and both confuse when the reader cannot tell which is happening. Say “Following your reading” or “Pinned · Go to verse”.

  3. Markers are an invitation, not a menu

    Every link is a decision, and a review of 38 hypertext experiments found that the extra load falls hardest on readers with less background knowledge. Mark verses with help quietly, as Spark Bible does on Psalm 23:4, and let people switch markers off, as Spark and Mere Bible do.

  4. Whose voice is beside the text?

    Medieval glossed Bibles set the commentary around the text in a smaller hand; the King James translators kept interpretive notes out of the margin altogether. On screen, name the kind of resource, its author and date, and where readings differ, as Matthew Henry and the Cambridge Bible do on verse 4, show more than one.

  5. A clip is part of a whole

    A minute of a sermon can mean something different from the sermon. Show where the clip sits, offer what came before, and caption it. Spark Bible’s timestamped transcripts are useful, but its automatic transcription is visibly wrong in places (“Full Or Theological Seminary”); correct a transcript before anyone quotes it.

Where to start

Which fits your product?

  • IfMost help is short: footnotes, cross-references, a first look

    Start withA preview card under the verse, with the full resource one tap away

  • IfPeople read commentary or watch teaching on a phone

    Start withA sheet that leaves the verse in sight and expands on request

  • IfPeople study for long stretches on larger screens

    Start withA study panel that says whether it follows the reading or is pinned

  • IfResources are long or need full attention

    Start withA full page whose Back returns to the same verse and shows it

  • IfThe app is mainly for devotional reading

    Start withNo markers by default; help on request, from one clear place

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What help is for

The Levites read the Law, “explaining it and giving insight, so that the people could understand what was being read” (Nehemiah 8:8). The Ethiopian asked, “How can I … unless someone guides me?” (Acts 8:31). The risen Jesus “explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself” (Luke 24:27). Help is part of how Scripture is received, and the Bereans still “examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true” (Acts 17:11).

Where Christians differ

Catholic canon law allows translations prepared with other Christians to be published “provided with appropriate annotations”. The King James translators allowed no interpretive notes at all, after the Geneva Bible’s notes were judged partisan. Protestant traditions affirm that Scripture is clear enough to read without an interpreter, while valuing teachers; Catholic and Orthodox traditions read Scripture within the church’s teaching.

What follows for design

Every note carries a voice and a tradition. Name the author, date and tradition, keep the help visibly beside the text and not inside it, and always return the reader to the verse. Offering more than one voice respects readers as Bereans who can weigh what they hear.

In conversation with

  • Augustine, On Christian Doctrine I.36.40 held that whoever interprets Scripture in a way that does not “build up this twofold love of God and our neighbor, does not yet understand them as he ought.”
  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) asks for editions “provided with suitable footnotes”, even for readers outside the church: help is part of access.

Edge cases to design for

Start with a short preview anchored to the verse, with the full resource one tap away. For longer help, use a sheet that leaves the verse visible, or a study panel on larger screens, and say whether the panel follows the reading or is pinned. If help takes the whole screen, make Back return to the same verse and show it. Mark verses with resources quietly, and let readers turn markers off. Show the kind of resource, its author and date, and where several voices disagree, show more than one. For media, show where the clip sits in the whole, offer what came before, and provide captions and a corrected transcript.

What tends to go wrong

A commentary styled like Scripture; no author or date; markers on every verse; autoplaying video between verses; a panel that changes without saying so; Back that returns to the top of the chapter; a 30-second clip presented as the speaker’s whole view; automatic transcripts quoted uncorrected; locked resources shown as if available.

How to test it

Ask readers to open help on a verse, read it and return; note whether they are on the same verse. Ask who wrote the help and when. Scroll with a following panel and ask why it changed. Watch a clip, then the two minutes before, and ask whether the meaning changed. Test with captions, a screen reader and 200% text.

Related decision: Preview before deep study

Reveal relevant information from a passage without replacing the reader.

Guidance

Offer a small selection action menu, then preview the relevant resource before deeper study. Let a linked panel either follow the passage or remain pinned, with its current anchor visible.

What tends to go wrong

A panel silently following a different verse; a resource opened without source context; a crowded selection menu that prevents reading.

How to test it

Test moving between verses with a linked and pinned panel. Can readers identify the panel’s anchor and recover the main reading task?

Related decision: How visible should extra resources be?

Compare inline cards, small markers and resources revealed on demand.

Guidance

Choose the visibility of supplementary material by the reading task. Keep it clearly separate from Scripture, with author and type visible. Offer a quiet reading mode. A hidden-on-selection design still needs a discoverable Resources action and keyboard and assistive-technology access.

What tends to go wrong

A teaching card mistaken for part of the translation; unexplained icons; autoplay between verses; a gesture-only resource menu; locked material presented as already available.

How to test it

Ask a new reader to find supplementary content, identify its author, hide it and resume at the same verse. Compare continuous reading with an intentional study task; include unavailable and paid resources.

Related decision: Original media before synthesis

Let readers inspect the source behind an explanation.

Guidance

Use media cards with creator, original source, duration and relevant time range. Link supported claims to precise excerpts. Offer transcript and original context; playback starts deliberately and failure leaves the conversation readable.

What tends to go wrong

Autoplay; incorrect time windows; edited clips that reverse meaning; source opinions presented as consensus; a missing video hidden behind a confident synthesis.

How to test it

Check whether every evidence claim is supported at the linked position. Test unavailable media, captions, returning from playback and the context before and after an excerpt.

Faith and interpretation

Scripture itself shows readers helped beside the text: the Levites “read from the Book of the Law of God, explaining it and giving insight, so that the people could understand what was being read” (Nehemiah 8:8), and the Ethiopian asked, “How can I … unless someone guides me?” (Acts 8:31). The medieval Glossa Ordinaria set the text in the centre of the page and the commentary around it, in a smaller hand. The King James translators allowed no interpretive notes in the margin, only notes on words and cross-references, because the Geneva Bible’s notes were thought partisan. Where help sits, and whose voice it carries, has always been a theological question as well as a layout one.
