Reading plans

What happens when someone misses days?

A reading plan decides what time means: a date to keep, a next step whenever the reader is ready, a number for the week, or the readings that belong to today. Life always interrupts, and the plan then decides whether coming back feels like a welcome or a bill. Decide what the plan asks of time, and what it says after days away. A plan is a gift to someone who wants to keep a rhythm; it should never become a debt.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    The plan’s author wanted readers free

    Robert Murray M’Cheyne warned that his own calendar could become “a yoke too heavy to bear”, and told readers to “throw aside the fetter”. Benedict told anyone who found his weekly psalter unsatisfactory to arrange it better. Build that freedom in: pause, own pace and moving dates.

  2. 2

    A missed day does not undo a habit

    In a study of people forming new daily habits, missing one opportunity did not materially affect how automatic the habit became. A plan that treats a missed day as failure misreads how habits work.

  3. 3

    Broken streaks drive people away

    Across seven studies, showing people a broken streak reduced their later engagement, most when they blamed themselves. Shame tends to make people hide and withdraw, guilt to repair. Avoid counts and copy that make a reader feel they have failed.

  4. 4

    Dates are for reading together

    Shared dates let a church or group read the same passage on the same day. That is worth keeping for group plans, with open days left unmarked and a choice on return. For personal reading, a sequence or a weekly number that starts fresh each Monday removes the debt altogether, as ESV’s “Read at My Own Pace” begins to do.

  5. 5

    Begin again on a landmark

    People start goals after temporal landmarks: a new week, a new month. A plan can offer a fresh start on Monday, or simply today’s reading, without first listing what was missed.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What a rhythm is for

“The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath” (Mark 2:27). Daily offices, lectionaries and the Moravian daily texts give each day its words so that no one has to choose alone. God’s mercies are “new every morning” (Lamentations 3:22–23): a rule of reading exists to bring people back to that newness, not to measure them.

Where Christians differ

Liturgical churches read by a shared calendar, so a congregation hears the same texts on the same day. Many evangelicals favour personal plans, often reading through the whole Bible in a year. Some value discipline as a means of grace; others warn that a plan can become a law. Dallas Willard distinguished effort from earning: a chosen discipline is not merit.

What follows for design

M’Cheyne warned that his own calendar could become “a yoke too heavy to bear”. Design the plan so that coming back is grace, not debt: no streaks to lose, no reminders that escalate, and a welcome that offers today’s reading without a bill for the days missed.

In conversation with

  • James K. A. Smith, You Are What You Love (2016) argues that habits are “liturgies” that train what we love; a streak is a small liturgy, and it teaches something about God and ourselves.
  • Dallas Willard, “Spiritual Disciplines and Means of Grace” (2002) insisted that “grace is not opposed to effort, but to earning”: a plan is effort, never merit.

Edge cases to design for

Decide what the plan is for. For reading together, keep shared dates but treat open days as available, not overdue, and on return offer today’s reading, where they stopped, or moving their own dates. For personal reading, use a sequence that waits, or a weekly number that starts fresh. For daily texts, let each day stand on its own. Explain what each change will do before it happens, keep completed readings and notes, let people pause without reminders, and say nothing about absence beyond what helps them choose.

What tends to go wrong

“You are 9 days behind”; a catch-up list of every missed reading; streak counters on the home screen; streak repair for money or gems; reminders that escalate after silence; resetting the plan and erasing history; group dashboards that show who is behind; asking why someone was away.

How to test it

Ask people returning after a week, a month and a year what they would do next and how the screen makes them feel. Try each change (pause, move dates, own pace) and ask what will happen before confirming. Test with a group plan: can someone fall back without the group seeing it?

Related decision: A flexible reading rhythm

Help someone choose a reading rhythm—and adjust it when life changes.

Guidance

Let the person choose between a dated plan and a sequence that waits for them. Offer reminders separately. Before pausing or moving dates, explain what will change and preserve completed sessions, notes and the saved place. For a group plan, distinguish a personal adjustment from a change to the shared calendar.

What tends to go wrong

Forced daily goals; erasing progress after rescheduling; presenting missed days as moral failures.

How to test it

Study real reading rhythms, including irregular and shared practice. Can users change pace without losing their place or feeling penalised?

Related decision: Return without shame

Make it easy to pick up a reading practice after time away.

Guidance

Show the saved place and a clear way to continue. In a dated plan, distinguish resuming from opening today’s reading. Make a different starting point available without erasing earlier work. Keep paused reminders paused unless the person chooses to restart them. A return should not require an explanation or a new commitment.

What tends to go wrong

A guilt message; demanding reasons for absence; “save your streak” payments; escalating reminder frequency after silence.

How to test it

Compare returning-user experiences through interviews and longitudinal observation. Does the welcome respect choice rather than merely increase opens?

Faith and interpretation

Christians have long read Scripture by rule and by calendar: the daily office, lectionaries and the Moravian watchwords give each day its texts. Rules served the reader. Benedict asked that the whole psalter be prayed each week, yet told anyone who found his arrangement unsatisfactory to arrange whatever he judged better. Robert Murray M’Cheyne, whose 1842 calendar is still widely used, warned that it might become “a yoke too heavy to bear”: “throw aside the fetter, and feed at liberty in the sweet garden of God.” His mercies are “new every morning” (Lamentations 3:22–23); a plan should be too.
