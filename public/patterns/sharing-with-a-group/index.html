Sharing with a group

When someone shares a prayer request, who hears it, and who looks after it?

Someone asks others to pray: for a mother’s surgery, for a new job, for something they have never said aloud. A prayer app decides who hears it, from one friend to everyone in the app, and what comes back: a heart, a verse, a stranger’s reply, or a person who knows what to do. Most requests are ordinary, and being prayed for by people who know you is a gift. Some are not, and the people closest to a worry are not always the right ones to hear it first. Paul asks the church to “rejoice with those who rejoice; weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15). Design for both.

Why these settings?

The ordinary request is about a mother’s surgery. In the hard situation a parent is worried about how a youth helper messages their daughter and doesn’t know who to tell; in several options a reply quotes Matthew 18:15 back, as that passage has been used to send people to confront someone privately first. When a church sets the app up, it names its safeguarding contact, someone outside the youth team, and says before anyone shares that church workers may have to pass a concern on. The independent route stays available either way.

1. Between you and God

Leaving sharing out. Hallow’s help centre describes the private setting: an intention “will only be saved to your journal and not shared with others”. Here the whole app works that way and points people to those they know and to their own church.

Try this

Write the request and keep it. Switch to the hard situation and open “Who can I talk to?”.

+ Makes easier

Nothing to leak, misread or answer badly.

− Costs

No one prays with them, and no one hears what is hard.

Borrowed from careful church practice outside apps: one person receives a request, agrees what is passed on and names no one without consent; and from safeguarding roles that sit apart from leadership, such as a Church of England parish’s lay safeguarding officer. Not observed in an app. Names, replies and the church are written for this example.

What we noticed

Takeaways

  1. Say who will hear it, before it is written

    Hallow’s requests go to friends, a church or a group, can’t be anonymous or edited, and stay active for 7 days; its help centre suggests writing “For a special intention” to keep details private. Show the audience, how long a request stays and whether it can be changed before someone starts typing, and ask before naming anyone else.

  2. Some listeners have to pass things on

    In Germany’s Protestant churches, church workers, volunteers included, must report a justified suspicion of sexualised violence to the church’s reporting office; pastoral confidentiality and the seal of confession are left untouched. A group leader may not be able to keep a secret, and shouldn’t promise to. Say so before someone shares, and say what happens next.

  3. Name the safeguarding contact

    When a church sets an app up, show its safeguarding contact with a way to reach them from inside the app, as a parish shows it on the noticeboard. Choose someone outside the leadership a concern might be about. German Catholic institutions must have a person trained in prevention; a Church of England parish must appoint a safeguarding officer, who should be a lay person.

  4. Keep an independent route beside the church’s

    Some people will not tell anyone at their church first, and sometimes they are right. Offer an independent service next to the church’s contact and say what each does with what it hears. The church route is often the right one; it should never be the only one.

  5. A verse quoted back can wound

    A reply such as “go and confront him privately” (Matthew 18:15) can send someone alone to the person they are afraid of. Groups can agree how they answer: listen first, pray, and offer verses only when asked.

    When Scripture is used to harm →

  6. Friends are not always the safest listeners

    A circle of friends is a gift for ordinary requests. For something hard, reactions from people close to us can be the most negative, so offer a trained listener beside them. Soultime nudges friends automatically “when we think you might need a call”, switched on; ask each time instead.

  7. Leave out what ranks prayer

    Hearts, counts and streak boards turn requests into content. A board with Praise and Remind buttons ranks people by how often they pray, and puts someone’s need underneath.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Being with someone

  • Weep with those who weep

    “Rejoice with those who rejoice; weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15). Being with someone comes before fixing them, and a reply box can make room for that.

  • Silence before answers

    Job’s friends “sat on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights, but no one spoke a word to him because they saw how intense his suffering was” (Job 2:13). Their trouble began when they started to explain.

  • In secret and together

    Jesus taught his disciples to pray behind a shut door (Matthew 6:6), and the first church prayed together (Acts 2:42). Both are prayer, so neither should be the only setting an app offers.

Church practice and church law

Who hears, and who must act

  • Ask before naming someone

    A United Methodist church suspended printed prayer requests after complaints about what they disclosed, then asked for written approval before praying for someone other than the person asking.

    SAmy Green

  • Some listeners must report

    The EKD’s guideline against sexualised violence (2019) obliges church workers, volunteers included, to report a justified suspicion to a reporting office, protecting their identity; pastoral confidentiality and the seal of confession stay untouched.

    SEvangelische Kirche in Deutschland

  • A trained person, and routes made known

    The German Catholic bishops’ framework for prevention, in force since January 2020, requires a person trained in prevention for every institution, and that advice and reporting routes be made known.

    SDeutsche Bischofskonferenz

  • A safeguarding officer apart from leadership

    In the Church of England, a parish must appoint at least one safeguarding officer, who should be a lay person, with contact details displayed in all church premises.

    SDiocese of Durham, Church of England

  • Members hidden from each other

    Planning Center’s confidential groups let support groups protect their members: “only group leaders will have access to the complete member list”.

    SJon Aleixo

Research and law

What sharing costs

  • Requests are special-category data

    A request names faith and often health, about the person and others. Under European data law both need an Article 9 condition, and inferences count too.

    SEuropean Data Protection BoardSUK Information Commissioner’s Office

  • How a listener reacts matters

    In a study of 228 women disclosing sexual assault, reactions from friends and family were more negative than those from police or service providers, and gave less practical help.

    RNational Institute of Justice

  • Groups help many people

    In Lifeway Research’s survey of US Protestant churchgoers, those in a small group five or more times a month scored 74.1 on a discipleship assessment, against 60.4 for those in none. A correlation, not proof that groups cause growth.

    RAaron Earls

  • Independent help has limits too

    thirtyone:eight shares information without consent only in exceptional cases, such as serious and immediate risk; Anlaufstelle.help is anonymous and passes people on to church offices only if they want.

    Sthirtyone:eightSRun for the EKD and Diakonie by the independent specialist service of Pfiffigunde Heilbronn e.V.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What sharing a burden is for

Paul asks the church to “carry one another’s burdens” (Galatians 6:2) and to “weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15); James to “pray for each other so that you may be healed” (James 5:16). Sharing a need is an act of trust, and hearing it is a charge: whoever listens becomes responsible for what they heard.

Where Christians differ

Catholic and Orthodox Christians confess sins to a priest, under a seal; many Protestant churches share requests openly in a prayer chain or a small group; Methodist class meetings and accountability groups ask members to tell each other how they are really doing. The same passages that call for bearing burdens have also been used to press people to disclose more than they chose, or to keep concerns about a leader inside the church.

What follows for design

Let people choose who hears them, and tell them before they share what each listener can and must do. Name a trained contact apart from the leadership, and keep an independent route beside it. Help groups listen before they answer, offer verses only when asked, and count nothing.

In conversation with

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) held that because a fellow Christian is Christ to me, I must listen to them as patiently as I listen to Christ, and bear their burdens as I share in his cross.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Make every request public and countable, notify friends automatically, and rank who prays most.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Show the audience before sharing, ask before naming others, let requests expire, and let people delete them.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Send something hard to one trained person instead of the feed, keep an independent route even when it takes people away from your community, and give up the engagement a public wall would bring.

Details

Go further

Edge cases to design for

Let the person choose the audience before writing, and show who will see the request, for how long and whether it can be edited or deleted. Ask before naming someone else. Offer a route to one chosen person, with what each listener can and must do shown before sending. When a church sets the app up, name its safeguarding contact and keep an independent service beside it. Give groups ways to respond that start with listening, keep support groups’ members confidential, and send no automatic notifications about someone without asking them each time.

What tends to go wrong

A public feed as the default; first names and requests visible to strangers; a request about someone else posted without their consent; friends notified automatically when the app infers a low mood; a leader as the only route for a concern about the leader’s team; verses quoted back at someone who disclosed harm; hearts and counts on requests; streak boards with Praise and Remind buttons; promising confidentiality a listener cannot keep.

How to test it

Ask people to share an ordinary request and something hard, then to name everyone who will see each, for how long, and what each listener must do with it. Read the replies a group gives to a hard request. Find the safeguarding contact and an independent service from inside the app in under a minute. Check what notifications say, and to whom.

Faith and interpretation

Paul asks the church to “rejoice with those who rejoice; weep with those who weep” (Romans 12:15), and to “carry one another’s burdens” (Galatians 6:2). Job’s friends did best when they “sat on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights, but no one spoke a word to him because they saw how intense his suffering was” (Job 2:13). Hearing a request is a charge as well as a gift. The same passages have been used to press people to share more than they chose, or to keep concerns inside a church, so the listener’s care matters as much as the sharer’s trust.
