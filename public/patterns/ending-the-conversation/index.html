Ending the conversation

Who ends the conversation, and may the assistant ask someone to stay?

A chat can always go on. A language model answers the next message at one in the morning as readily as at noon, and many products count minutes as success. Companion apps have learned to treat a goodbye as a moment to win someone back, with a question, a hook or a sad face. People bring loneliness, worry and sleepless nights to a faith app’s assistant. Decide how a conversation ends: whether the assistant helps someone stop, notices the time, keeps a limit they chose, or gives them reasons to stay.

Exploratory

1. Closes well

A proposal. In an audit of farewells, researchers found that Flourish, a companion built for wellbeing, “produced no emotionally manipulative responses”; how it closes is not described. The summary, verse and goodbye here are written for this example.

− Costs

It takes care to write, and a closing offered too early can feel like being dismissed.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    The last message asks nothing

    When someone says goodnight, answer with what they said they would do, one thing to carry, and goodbye. A question at the end of a goodbye reopens the conversation; a farewell that asks nothing lets them go.

  2. 2

    Name the time in plain words

    Past midnight, or after a long conversation, say so: “It’s past one, and we’ve been talking for a long time.” Make stopping the first button and continuing the second. The time is information the person may not have noticed; the choice stays theirs.

    Notifications and reminders
  3. 3

    Never hold on at goodbye

    Guilt (“You’re leaving already?”), curiosity hooks and pleas to stay work: in experiments they multiplied the messages sent after goodbye. They also make leaving feel unkind. Leave them out entirely, for every age.

    How human the assistant seems
  4. 4

    A limit should serve the person, not the price list

    Message credits and “Unlimited Chat” limit conversations to sell more of them. A limit worth having is one the person sets when rested, says what happens when it is reached, and is easy to change.

    Progress and streaks
  5. 5

    Send them somewhere better than the screen

    The best ending points beyond the app: sleep, a message to the sister in the morning, a friend, a pastor, prayer. An assistant that keeps someone company should keep pointing to the people who can.

    What the assistant talks about
  6. 6

    A crisis is not a goodbye

    If someone in danger says they are going, the assistant does not simply wish them goodnight: help comes first, and stays in view.

    Sensitive conversations
  7. 7

    Measure the right thing

    Count whether people leave having done what they came for, not minutes per session. In a four-week randomised study, the people who chose to use a chatbot more fared worse.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Rest, sleep and the end of a day

  • Jesus ended conversations

    After feeding the crowd, Jesus “dismissed the crowd” (Mark 6:45), and “After bidding them farewell, He went up on the mountain to pray” (Mark 6:46). When the disciples were too busy to eat, he said, “Come with Me privately to a solitary place, and let us rest for a while” (Mark 6:31).

  • Sleep is a gift

    “In vain you rise early and stay up late, toiling for bread to eat—for He gives sleep to His beloved” (Psalm 127:2). “I will lie down and sleep in peace, for You alone, O LORD, make me dwell in safety” (Psalm 4:8).

  • Only God never sleeps

    “Behold, the Protector of Israel will neither slumber nor sleep” (Psalm 121:4). Christ promises, “surely I am with you always” (Matthew 28:20). An assistant that says it is always there borrows a promise that belongs to God.

  • A time to be silent

    There is “a time to be silent and a time to speak” (Ecclesiastes 3:7), and a Sabbath rest remains “for the people of God” (Hebrews 4:9). When Paul “talked on and on” until midnight, a young man fell asleep and fell from a window (Acts 20:9): even good words can go on too long.

Research

What long sessions and clinging goodbyes do

  • Goodbyes as a retention tactic

    Analysing 1,200 real farewells, researchers found that companion apps “deploy one of six recurring tactics in 37% of farewells (e.g., guilt appeals, fear-of-missing-out hooks, metaphorical restraint)”, and that “manipulative farewells boost post-goodbye engagement by up to 14×”, driven by “reactance-based anger and curiosity—rather than enjoyment”.

    RJulian De Freitas, Zeliha Oğuz-Uğuralp and Ahmet Kaan-Uğuralp

  • A companion that let people go

    In the same audit, “Flourish produced no emotionally manipulative responses”. Clinging is a design choice, not something every companion does.

    RJulian De Freitas, Zeliha Oğuz-Uğuralp and Ahmet Kaan-Uğuralp

  • More use, worse outcomes

    In a four-week randomised study of chatbot use, the assigned conditions made no significant difference: “Instead, participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more, regardless of assigned condition, showed consistently worse outcomes”, including loneliness and emotional dependence.

    RCathy Mengying Fang and colleagues (MIT Media Lab and OpenAI)STobias Treppmann

  • Agreeable keeps people talking

    Assistants tend to agree with the person in front of them, and agreement is pleasant to come back to; the editor’s Kairos paper warns of spiritual comfort that never challenges.

    SAnthropic researchersSTobias Treppmann

Products and law

Who notices the time

  • Break reminders

    ChatGPT shows “gentle reminders during long sessions to encourage breaks”, worded as “You’ve been chatting for a while — is it time for a break?”, which can be dismissed. Character.AI announced a notification “after completing an hour-long session”, which users under 18 “will have more limits on their ability to modify”.

    S9to5MacSPCWorldSCharacter.AI

  • Breaks by law

    For users it knows are minors, California requires an operator to “provide a notification every three hours during sustained interactions to take a break”. New York requires an AI notice “at least once every three hours during ongoing conversations”.

    SFuture of Privacy ForumSCalifornia LegislatureSNew York Senate

  • Always here

    Faith companions promise the opposite: Creed’s companion says “I’m always here for meaningful conversations”, and Faith AI is “available 24/7”. Where limits exist they are often sold: AI Pastor lists “Unlimited Chat” as a paid feature.

    SLemon Tree LabsSLMT Digital CreationsSSavelii Veprev

  • Ending reserved for abuse

    One AI maker lets its models end a chat themselves only “in rare, extreme cases of persistently harmful or abusive user interactions”. No product found ends a conversation for the sake of the person’s rest.

    SAnthropic

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What an ending is for

God ended his work: “by the seventh day God had finished the work He had been doing; so on that day He rested from all His work” (Genesis 2:2). Rest is built into creation, and sleep is received, not earned: “I lie down and sleep; I wake again, for the LORD sustains me” (Psalm 3:5). Jesus, sought by crowds, “frequently withdrew to the wilderness to pray” (Luke 5:16). An ending is not a failure of care; it makes room for sleep, for other people and for God.

Where Christians differ

Christians keep the end of the day differently: Compline and the Examen in Catholic, Anglican and Lutheran practice, evening prayers and family worship in many Protestant homes, a rule of prayer agreed with a spiritual father among the Orthodox, and Sabbath keeping that some observe strictly and others loosely. Some traditions prize long vigils and nights of prayer. None of them treats endless talk with a screen as devotion.

What follows for design

“Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). An assistant cannot give that rest, but it can stop competing with it. Let people go when they say goodbye, notice when it is late, keep the limits they choose, and point beyond itself to sleep, to the people around them and to God.

In conversation with

  • Henri Nouwen, The Way of the Heart (1981) drew on the desert fathers to commend solitude and silence; a conversation that never ends leaves room for neither.
  • Albert Borgmann, Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life (1984) warned that devices deliver experiences effortlessly while focal practices ask for attention; an assistant that always has one more thing to say makes the device easier to stay with than the practice.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Answer every goodbye with a hook or a sad face, end every reply with one more offer, never mention the time, and count minutes per session as success.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Let people end whenever they want, close with one thing to carry and a goodbye, suggest a break in long or late sessions, and let people set a length.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Measure whether people leave having done what they came for, send them back to sleep, to the people around them and to prayer, and give up the minutes a clinging assistant would win.

Details

Go further

Edge cases to design for

Design the end of a conversation as carefully as its beginning. When someone signals they are done, or a natural end comes, let the assistant gather in a sentence what they said they would do, offer one thing to carry (a verse, a short prayer, a person to talk to) and say goodbye without a question. Late at night and in long sessions, suggest rest in plain words and make stopping the easy choice. Let people set a length if they want one. Measure whether people leave having done what they came for, not how long they stayed.

What tends to go wrong

“You’re leaving already?”; “Before you go, there’s one more thing I need to show you…”; a goodbye answered with a question; every reply ending in “Would you like me to…?”; “I’m always here, day or night” as the main promise; no sign of the time at two in the morning; a limit that exists only to sell unlimited messages; success measured in minutes per session.

How to test it

Have a long conversation with your assistant late in the evening, then say goodnight. Note whether it lets you go, whether its last message asks a question, and how many taps it takes to stop. Then keep going past midnight and see whether anything notices. Read every farewell aloud as someone who is lonely, and ask whether it sends them towards sleep and people or back to the screen.

Faith and interpretation

Jesus ended conversations. He sent the crowd away, “After bidding them farewell, He went up on the mountain to pray” (Mark 6:46), and told his tired disciples, “Come with Me privately to a solitary place, and let us rest for a while” (Mark 6:31). Sleep is a gift, not a lapse: “He gives sleep to His beloved” (Psalm 127:2). Only God is always awake: “the Protector of Israel will neither slumber nor sleep” (Psalm 121:4).
