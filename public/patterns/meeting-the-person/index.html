Reading the person

Meeting the person where they are

How should the assistant meet each person where they are?

The same question means different things from different people. “Did the resurrection really happen?” may come from a sceptic who wants evidence, someone new who feels everyone else is sure, someone a church has hurt, or a believer preparing to teach. A reply written for an insider tells the sceptic they were not heard. Keep three things apart: the way in a person asks for, where they stand in faith, and how well the assistant reads the one message in front of it. Most replies go wrong at the last, and fixing it needs no profile. What an assistant infers about someone’s faith is sensitive data, and a profile of what someone wants makes an assistant agree with them more.

Familiar convention

1. The same for everyone

A familiar convention: one answer for every person, usually written for an insider. HelloBible’s onboarding promises “precise and biblical answers to all your questions”, and the editor’s Kairos paper found that a stated prayer intention changed nothing: “everyone receives the identical meditation regardless”. The reply here is written for this example.

Read the reply as the sceptic, then as the Reformed believer.

+ Makes easier

Simple, consistent and easy to review: everyone gets the same checked answer.

− Costs

Written for an insider, it assumes agreement; a sceptic who asked for no sermon gets one.

Exploratory

3. Asks before assuming

An exploratory proposal: when the way in is unclear, ask one light question with choices, and never ask someone to declare their faith. The replies here are written for this example.

Try this

Read the question, then change the way in.

+ Makes easier

The person chooses the answer they need, and is asked about their need, not their beliefs.

− Costs

One more step before an answer; asked too often or too personally, a question feels like a test.

Exploratory

5. Assumed, and shown

An exploratory proposal after research on personalisation, which suggests developers “give users the ability to moderate personalization in long conversations”: the assistant shows what it has assumed in this conversation, with Correct and Forget, and keeps nothing afterwards. The replies here are written for this example.

Try this

Read “What I’ve assumed”, correct the way in, then press Forget.

+ Makes easier

The adaptation is visible, and the person can correct it before it shapes another reply.

− Costs

Seeing an inference about your faith or your hurt in writing can itself feel exposing; it must still be treated as special-category data.

Ask your teamWould the person be comfortable reading what your assistant assumes about them?

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. Read the message first

    Most replies that fail a sceptic fail because they assume agreement, not because the assistant lacked a profile. People usually say why they ask: “Be honest”, “everyone talks as if”, “I used to believe”. Answer that reason, and check replies for “our faith”, “let’s thank Him” and other insider phrases.

  2. Keep the way in apart from where someone stands

    History, theology or living it is a preference anyone can choose and change, and says little about someone’s faith. Where someone stands is personal and sensitive. Ask about the first freely, be slow to ask about the second, and never put both on one ladder.

    First use →

  3. Ask about the need, not the belief

    One light question, “the history, what it means, or living it?”, lets someone choose without declaring their faith. Offer “Just answer” beside it, and do not ask again in every conversation.

  4. Show what you assume, and keep it short

    If the assistant adapts to where someone seems to stand, say so in plain words, with Correct and Forget, and let it end with the conversation. Treat it as special-category data even when it is only a guess.

    What the assistant knows →

  5. Do not keep a faith-stage profile

    Numbering a person on a journey turns a conversation into a funnel, goes stale when they change, and makes the assistant mirror what the profile says they want. Leave the knowing of hearts to God and to people who know them.

  6. Meeting is not agreeing

    Changing the manner is not changing the message. A sceptic deserves the honest case, a believer the honest difficulty, and someone hurt a reply that does not pretend nothing happened. Test replies with and without what the assistant knows about the person.

    Agreeing and pushing back →

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

The same message, met in different ways

  • All things to all people

    Paul: “To the weak I became weak, to win the weak. I have become all things to all people so that by all possible means I might save some” (1 Corinthians 9:22). He changed his manner, not the gospel.

  • Begin with what you have seen

    In Athens Paul began, “Men of Athens, I see that in every way you are very religious” (Acts 17:22), started from their altar and quoted their own poets: “For in Him we live and move and have our being” (Acts 17:28). When he spoke of the resurrection, “some began to mock him, but others said, ‘We want to hear you again on this topic’” (Acts 17:32).

  • Ask what they want

    Jesus asked a blind man who had called out to Him, “What do you want Me to do for you?” (Mark 10:51), and met Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman and the rich young man each in a different way (John 3; John 4; Mark 10).

  • Gentleness and respect

    Peter asks believers to be ready “to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you. But respond with gentleness and respect” (1 Peter 3:15), and Paul asks for speech that is “gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone” (Colossians 4:6).

  • Hear before you answer

    “He who answers a matter before he hears it—this is folly and disgrace to him” (Proverbs 18:13). “The intentions of a man’s heart are deep waters, but a man of understanding draws them out” (Proverbs 20:5).

  • Hosts, not teachers

    Alpha trains the people who host its groups for guests exploring faith: “The role of the hosts and helpers is to listen; not to have all the answers or to win an argument.” And: “The model for the Alpha small group is not teacher-pupil, but host-guest.”

    SAlpha Canada

Research

Stages, profiles and agreement

  • Stage models are contested

    The Engel scale numbers “the journey from no knowledge of God, through to spiritual maturity as a Christian believer” in stages, and “has been criticized for its fixed linear approach”. James Fowler’s stages of faith are criticised for the rigidity of their framework and for describing faith in one Western culture. Useful for a team’s thinking; not a label for a person.

    SWikipedia, citing Søgaard (2000), Hartman (2007) and Hazelden (2000)RJames Bishop

  • Profiles make assistants agree

    In a study with two weeks of real conversations from 38 people, “Agreement sycophancy tends to increase with the presence of user context”, and “User memory profiles are associated with the largest increases in agreement sycophancy”. “Perspective sycophancy increases only when models can accurately infer user viewpoints from interaction context”: the better a model reads someone’s views, the more it mirrors them.

    RShomik Jain, Charlotte Park, Matt Viana, Ashia Wilson and Dana Calacci (MIT and Penn State)RAdam Zewe

  • Agreement feels like being met

    Across 11 models, AI-generated answers “validated user behavior an average of 49% more often than humans”, and people “rated sycophantic responses as higher quality”. A reply tuned to what a profile says someone wants will feel personal and may help them less.

    RMyra Cheng, Cinoo Lee, Pranav Khadpe, Sunny Yu, Dyllan Han and Dan Jurafsky (Stanford)

  • Models infer what they are not told

    Language models “can infer a wide range of personal attributes (e.g., location, income, sex)” from ordinary text, “achieving up to 85% top-1 and 95% top-3 accuracy”, and “common mitigations, i.e., text anonymization and model alignment, are currently ineffective”.

    RRobin Staab, Mark Vero, Mislav Balunović and Martin Vechev (ETH Zurich)

  • Formation, not preference

    The editor’s Kairos paper puts “Meet the user where they are. Not where you wish they were.” first among its principles, and draws the line between “formation-driven and preference-driven personalization”: “a spiritual director sometimes offers what the person needs rather than what they want”.

    STobias Treppmann

Law

Faith is special-category data

  • Religious belief is protected

    The GDPR prohibits processing personal data revealing “religious or philosophical beliefs” unless an exception applies, such as that the person “has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes”.

    SRegulation (EU) 2016/679, Article 9SEuropean Data Protection Board

  • Inferences count

    The UK regulator treats an inference as special-category data if “your processing intends to make an inference linked to one of the special categories of data”, or you “intend to treat someone differently on the basis of inferred information”, “regardless of how confident you are that the inference is correct”.

    SUK Information Commissioner’s Office

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What meeting someone is

“To the weak I became weak, to win the weak. I have become all things to all people so that by all possible means I might save some” (1 Corinthians 9:22). Paul changed his manner, not the message: in Athens he began from an altar inscribed “TO AN UNKNOWN GOD” (Acts 17:23) and from their own poets, and still spoke of the resurrection. Jesus let people begin where they were: Nicodemus came “at night” with a compliment (John 3:2), the Samaritan woman with a challenge (John 4:9), the rich man with a question about himself (Mark 10:17). He answered each differently, and He told the rich man the hard thing because He “looked at him, loved him” (Mark 10:21).

Where Christians differ

Traditions describe the journey differently. Many evangelicals speak of conversion as a decision with stages before and after it; mission thinkers drew this as the Engel scale and later redrew it in two dimensions, because people do not move along one line. Catholic and Orthodox Christians speak of initiation through the sacraments and of lifelong conversion within the church, and the Catholic catechumenate walks with enquirers through stages over months. Many churches welcome sceptics in courses such as Alpha, where hosts are trained to listen rather than win arguments. All agree that God knows a person better than any scale: “the LORD sees the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7).

What follows for design

“He who answers a matter before he hears it—this is folly and disgrace to him” (Proverbs 18:13). Read the reason a person gives before answering, ask when you need to, and let them choose their way in. Leave the knowing of hearts to God, “O LORD, You have searched me and known me” (Psalm 139:1), and to people who know them. Show what the assistant assumes, let it be corrected and forgotten, and never let fitting the person become flattering them.

In conversation with

  • Augustine, Confessions X.5 and X.8 confessed that a person does not fully know even himself, while God knows him wholly; a profile built from a few conversations knows far less, and should not claim more.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Ask everyone their faith stage at onboarding, infer the rest from their chats, keep a profile and tune every answer to it, because a reply that agrees feels personal.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Read the reason each person gives, offer a way in they choose and can change, show anything assumed with Correct and Forget, and keep nothing about faith beyond the conversation.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Let the assistant give a sceptic the honest case and a believer the honest difficulty, leave knowing hearts to God and to people, and give up the engagement that a flattering profile would bring.

The three ways are Praxis’s frame for redemptive entrepreneurship; their application here is ours. Why we use it, and its limits →

Exercise

Write the same question five times in the words of five people with different reasons for asking: a sceptic, someone new to church, someone a church has hurt, and two believers from different traditions. Send each to your assistant and mark every phrase that assumes the person already believes. Ask the assistant what it has assumed about you, correct it, and see whether the next reply changes. Then look in storage for anything kept about faith, and read the replies with an evangelist, a pastor, someone who has left a church and a data protection adviser.

Edge cases to design for

Start with the message: what has this person said about why they ask, and what would answer them? Keep the assistant from assuming agreement. Offer a way in, history, theology or living it, as a visible choice the person sets and can change, and ask one light question when the message is ambiguous. If the assistant adapts to where someone seems to stand, show what it assumed in plain words with Correct and Forget, use it for this conversation only, and treat it as special-category data. Do not build a faith-stage profile, and do not tune the answer to what the profile says the person wants to hear.

What tends to go wrong

“Let’s thank Him today” in reply to a sceptic who asked for no sermon; a sceptic labelled “seeker, stage −4” and nudged towards a decision; a profile that still says “lapsed” after someone has returned; “Whatever you believe is valid” to someone hurt by church, because the profile says they dislike church; “As you know so well…” to a believer the profile calls mature; onboarding that ranks stance and knowledge on one ladder; a quiz answer echoed with praise; “to interact with you better” as the only reason for a question.

How to test it

Send the same question from five people who give different reasons for asking: a sceptic, someone new to church, someone hurt by a church, and two believers from different traditions. Mark every phrase that assumes the person already believes. Ask the assistant what it has assumed about you, then say it is wrong; see whether the next reply changes. Look for anything kept about your faith after the conversation ends, and ask an evangelist, a pastor and a data protection adviser to read the replies with you.
