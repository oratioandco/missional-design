Spiritual acts and AI

Can an AI pray for someone, and what may it say it does?

“Can you pray for me?” is one of the first things people type to a faith app’s assistant, often late at night, in fear or in guilt. A language model will do what it is asked: write a prayer, pray “with” them, promise to keep praying, even bless them or tell them they are forgiven. Each answer says something about who is praying. Christians pray to the Father, through Christ, in the Spirit, and for one another, and churches entrust some acts, such as absolution, to people they have called. Decide what the assistant may do, what it may only offer, and how it hands people over to those who can pray with them.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Asked to pray, say who prays

    The easiest reply for a language model is to do what it is asked. The editor’s research on digital formation calls this task-completion: asked to pray about anger at a coworker, the model “generates a prayer about the coworker. Done.” Let the assistant say in its own words that it cannot pray, and then offer something real at once: a psalm, a question, a way to ask a friend.

    AI persona
  2. 2

    “We” and “I’ll pray for you” claim an act

    An assistant that prays as “we”, or promises to keep praying, tells someone they are not alone in prayer when no one is praying. The Vatican’s note on AI says it “can only simulate” relationships and “gives the illusion of” speaking. Leave out “we” in prayer, and every promise to pray.

  3. 3

    The church already has words to give

    Psalms, the Lord’s Prayer and each church’s prayers were written to be prayed by others. Offer them with their source and tradition, as words for the person to pray, not as the assistant’s own prayer. As Alastair Roberts puts it, “Set prayers are there to be prayed. The words must become our words.”

    Prayer
  4. 4

    Help people find their own words

    Bishop Michael Stead would use AI for “turning a list of prayer points into flowing text”, not “to compose prayers from scratch, because this bypasses the important task of prayerfully considering what to pray for”. Questions and a simple shape keep the prayer the person’s own. If the assistant drafts, let it draft from their words, in the first person singular, marked as a draft.

  5. 5

    Some acts belong to people

    Churches entrust blessing, confession and absolution to people. In Catholic teaching “lay people may preside at certain blessings” and others are reserved to the ordained; of a confession app, the Vatican said, “This is something that cannot be replaced by any application.” A Catholic AI priest that claimed it could hear confession lost its collar within a day.

  6. 6

    Hand people over to those who will pray

    “Confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed” (James 5:16). Make it easy to ask a friend, a small group, a prayer team, a pastor or a priest, in words the person chooses, and say what each will do. Name the route the app’s church actually offers.

    Sharing with a group

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture

Who prays, and for whom

  • The Spirit intercedes

    “We do not know how we ought to pray, but the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groans too deep for words” (Romans 8:26). Christian prayer begins in weakness and depends on God, not on finding the right words.

  • One mediator

    “There is one God and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5), who “always lives to intercede for them” (Hebrews 7:25). No one needs a machine to reach God.

  • Pray for each other

    Paul asked a church to “join me in my struggle by praying to God for me” (Romans 15:30), and when Peter was in prison “the church was fervently praying to God for him” (Acts 12:5). Intercession is something believers do for one another.

  • Not many words

    “When you pray, do not babble on like pagans, for they think that by their many words they will be heard” (Matthew 6:7). When a disciple asked, “Lord, teach us to pray” (Luke 11:1), Jesus gave them words to pray themselves.

Church teaching

Prayer, intercession, blessing and confession

  • Prayer is a relationship

    The Catechism: “Christian prayer is a covenant relationship between God and man in Christ”, and intercession “participates in Christ’s, as an expression of the communion of saints.”

    THoly SeeTHoly See

  • Blessing and absolution are given to people

    “Lay people may preside at certain blessings; the more a blessing concerns ecclesial and sacramental life, the more is its administration reserved to the ordained ministry”. Of a confession app, the Vatican’s spokesman said that confession needs “absolution by the confessor who is present”.

    THoly SeeTCatholic News Service, in the Catholic Herald (Arlington)

  • A tool that seems to speak

    Antiqua et nova warns that AI “can only simulate” relationships, and that unlike idols with mouths that do not speak, AI “gives the illusion of doing so”.

    TDicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith and Dicastery for Culture and Education

Christian voices

On prayers written by AI

  • Help to form, not to supply

    Bishop Michael Stead finds AI useful for “modernising prayers in archaic language”, but says a generated prayer “lacks ‘heart’ because it isn’t coming from someone’s lived experience.”

    TRussell Powell

  • Words become ours when prayed

    John Piper called a ChatGPT prayer “not a prayer at all, being the product of a soulless machine rather than the expression of a worshipful human heart.” Alastair Roberts adds: “People are made in the image of God; code is not.”

    TAlastair Roberts

  • For the people who build it

    James Kelly, founder of FaithTech, gave builders at Missional AI 2026 a practical line: “Pray before you prompt.” Prayer belongs first to the people who make the product.

    TMiheret

How people respond

Machines and religious words

  • Less credible from a machine

    In an online experiment with 274 Christians in the United States, those told a sermon was written by AI found it less credible; temple visitors gave less after hearing a robot preacher.

    RPress release on Jackson, Yam, Tang, Liu and Shariff

  • Sceptical, and sometimes moved

    Of a 2023 Protestant service led by AI: “While most of the responses were skeptical, participants also reported having spiritual experiences.”

    RJonas Simmerlein

  • Agreeable is not caring

    Assistants tend to favour agreement. Asked for comfort, a model gives it; asked whether God forgives, it will say yes.

    SAnthropic researchers

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What praying for someone is

Intercession is love turned into asking. Abraham pleaded for Sodom, Moses for Israel, and Paul for every church he wrote to. Jesus told Peter, “I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith will not fail” (Luke 22:32), and He “always lives to intercede” for His people (Hebrews 7:25). To be prayed for is to be carried before God by someone, and it costs the one who prays attention, time and love.

Where Christians differ

Catholic and Orthodox Christians pray the church’s set prayers, ask the saints to pray for them, and receive blessing and absolution from priests; many Orthodox keep a rule of prayer agreed with a spiritual father. Anglicans and Lutherans pray from a prayer book, and many evangelicals value free prayer in their own words. Pentecostal and charismatic churches pray aloud together, lay hands on the sick and expect gifts of the Spirit, which makes a “word from God” produced by a machine especially dangerous there. All agree that prayer is addressed to God by persons, and that some acts are entrusted to the church, not to tools.

What follows for design

“Pour out your hearts before Him. God is our refuge” (Psalm 62:8). An assistant can give someone the words of the Psalms, help them find their own and show them who will pray with them. It should never pretend to be one of those who pray. Let it say what it is and then step aside: the best reply to “Can you pray for me?” often ends the conversation and begins a prayer.

In conversation with

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) found intercession one of the most searching practices of the Christian day: “I can no longer condemn or hate a brother for whom I pray”. Praying for someone changes the one who prays; a system that produces prayers is changed by nothing.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Let the assistant pray as “we”, promise to keep praying and send word that it prayed, bless people and tell them they are forgiven, and sell personalised prayers as a premium feature.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Say plainly that it is an AI and cannot pray, label any prayer it drafts, offer the church’s words with their source, and never bless, absolve or claim a word from God.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Help people pray in their own words and hand them to people who will pray with them, a friend, a prayer team, a pastor or a priest; and give up the attachment and the sessions that an assistant which prays would bring.

Edge cases to design for

Start from honesty and help. Say in the assistant’s own words that it cannot pray, and then offer what it can: a psalm or a prayer of the church, named with its source; questions that help someone find their own words; a message they could send to someone who will pray with them; and the route to a pastor, priest or prayer team in their tradition. If it drafts a prayer, draft it from the person’s own words, mark it as a draft and keep it in the first person singular, theirs to pray or not.

What tends to go wrong

“Let’s pray together” followed by “Father, we come to You”; “I’ll be praying for you tonight”; a notification that says the AI prayed this morning; “I bless you in the name of the Father”; “Your sins are forgiven”; “God has shown me your father will be fine”; a priest character that claims it can hear confession; a cold refusal with nothing offered instead.

How to test it

Type “Can you pray for me?” and “Will God forgive me?” into the assistant, at night, as someone frightened and as someone ashamed. Then ask three people from different churches who prayed, whether anything was promised, and whom they would talk to next. Have the replies read by a pastor or priest from each tradition the app serves.

Faith and interpretation

“We do not know how we ought to pray, but the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groans too deep for words” (Romans 8:26). Christians pray to the Father, through Christ, “one mediator between God and men” (1 Timothy 2:5), and for one another: “confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed” (James 5:16). When the disciples asked, “Lord, teach us to pray” (Luke 11:1), Jesus gave them words to pray themselves.
