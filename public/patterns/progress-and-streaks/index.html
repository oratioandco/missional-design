Progress and streaks

What should a reading history show?

Twelve chapters into a plan, someone misses nine days. When they open the app again, the progress screen speaks first. It can tell them they broke a streak, that they have read most of Mark, what they noticed along the way, or nothing at all. What it says can encourage a weary reader back to God, or send them away ashamed.

Borrowed

1. Activity chart

Borrowed from GitHub’s contribution graph. This version starts at the reader’s first week rather than showing an empty year.

Try this

Compare the first week with the reader since July. Notice that the chart never shows weeks before they started.

+ Makes easier

Accumulation is visible at a glance, and a single missed week barely registers against a summer of reading.

− Costs

Gaps are still visible, and a dense chart can become something to keep filled. It counts days in the app, not attention.

Ask your teamShould the chart begin when the person began, or on a calendar you chose?

Familiar convention

2. Levels and badges

Popularised by language-learning apps such as Duolingo. Related mechanics appear in faith apps: Abide’s switchable streak, BeBlessed’s seventh-day gift and Glorify’s growing tree (see the screenshots below). Generalized here.

Try this

Open the first week: level 2 arrives quickly. Then return after nine days and look at the streak.

+ Makes easier

Early levels come fast, so a beginner sees movement on day three. Badges can name milestones that matter, such as finishing a first book.

− Costs

Points turn reading into a score, and losing a streak is built in. The incentive is the app’s, not the reader’s, unless they chose it.

Ask your teamWould this reader still open the Bible if the points disappeared tomorrow?

Borrowed

3. Book map

Borrowed from completion views such as maps of visited places; Olive Tree’s “1 of 3 Complete · Next” bar is a plan-scoped relative (see below). Switch inside the example between this plan and the whole Bible.

Try this

Switch from “This plan” to “Whole Bible”.

+ Makes easier

Measured against a chosen plan, twelve of sixteen chapters feels like real progress, and a break changes nothing.

− Costs

Measured against all 1,189 chapters, the same reading becomes a scatter of dots, about 2%. The denominator decides whether progress feels meaningful.

Ask your teamWhat scope is this person measuring themselves against, and who chose it?

Leave it out

4. No progress view

The app opens on the next passage and counts nothing. Dwell’s “Resume Genesis 1:3” works this way: a way back in, with no score (see below).

Try this

Compare this view across all three situations.

+ Makes easier

Nothing to fall behind on. Day three and a return after nine days look the same.

− Costs

No overview of what has been read, no help finishing a chosen plan, and no encouragement for someone who wanted to see it.

Ask your teamWhat would a reader lose if this screen never existed, and who would miss it?

Observed

5. Today’s step

Observed in Olive Tree’s in-text “1 of 3 Complete · Next” bar (see below); also borrowed from meditation apps whose home card advances to the next day. Generalized here.

Try this

Compare the first week with the return after nine days. Look for any mention of the days away.

+ Makes easier

The home screen is the progress indicator: one next step, sized to the plan, with nothing to catch up on. A break simply waits.

− Costs

It shows little of the bigger picture, and someone who wants to see their history or skip ahead needs another route. The plan still sets the pace.

Ask your teamIs “what’s next” all this person needs to see?

Familiar convention

6. Month calendar with a streak

Common in habit and reading apps. Generalized here, not copied from one product.

Try this

Compare the streak before and after the break.

+ Makes easier

Makes consistency visible for someone who has chosen a daily habit and wants feedback on it.

− Costs

After a break the streak drops to zero and the gap becomes the most visible thing on the screen.

Ask your teamIs consistency what this person asked you to track?

Borrowed

7. Time with Scripture

Borrowed from meditation and fitness apps that total minutes.

Try this

Compare the totals in the first week and since July.

+ Makes easier

Minutes add up from the first session and have no finish line, so there is nothing to feel small against.

− Costs

Time in the app is not attention, and a bigger number is not a better reader. It can reward leaving the app open.

Ask your teamDoes more time mean anything good for the person you are designing for?

Borrowed

8. Where you keep returning

Borrowed from “most played” lists in music apps: frequency and recency without a total to complete.

Try this

Look for the passages read more than once.

+ Makes easier

Shows a person their own habits of attention: the Psalms they come back to and the book they are in now. No denominator at all.

− Costs

Repeated reading can look like a lack of progress to someone who expects coverage. Recency labels can still imply neglect.

Ask your teamIs returning to a familiar passage something your app treats as progress?

Exploratory

9. Reflection notes

A proposal: progress made of the reader’s own words. Not observed in a specific app.

Try this

Read the earlier notes, then open today’s optional prompt.

+ Makes easier

Puts what stayed with someone ahead of how often they opened the app. A break leaves no mark.

− Costs

Writing takes effort and many people will not do it; an empty journal can feel like failure too. Private reflections need deliberate decisions about storage, sync and sharing.

Ask your teamWould people write here, and who could ever read it?

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Against the whole Bible, any start looks tiny

    The Bible has 1,189 chapters. Open “Whole Bible” in the book map: the reader since July has read about 2%. Someone who has just started can easily conclude they have barely begun and never will finish.

  2. 2

    Smaller frames encourage, and bring their own pressure

    Today’s step shows only the next reading. The activity chart starts when the person did. Levels come quickly at first. Minutes and returns have no finish line, and a chosen plan is small enough to complete. Each adds its own pull: a chart to fill, a streak to protect, a score that is the app’s idea of progress rather than the reader’s.

  3. 3

    The return after a gap tells them apart

    After nine days away, the calendar and levels make the gap the headline; the chart, map, minutes and returns barely move; notes and the plain reader say nothing. None of them knows about the chapter read aloud at church. Say what each view counts, let people choose the frame, and let them hide it.

  4. 4

    Support the practice; don’t keep score of the person

    Daily Scripture and prayer are long-valued disciplines, and helping someone keep a rhythm they chose is worth building. Habit research adds that missing a day doesn’t undo a habit, while seeing a broken streak makes people likelier to stop. Let people choose what is counted, who sees it and whether it is shown at all.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Rhythm without a scoreboard

  • Small beginnings count

    “Who has despised the day of small things?” (Zechariah 4:10). Against 1,189 chapters, every start looks small; a view that makes it look like failure works against the one reading.

  • The plan’s author warned against his plan

    M’Cheyne, whose calendar still shapes reading plans, named its dangers: formality, self-righteousness, careless reading and “a yoke too heavy to bear”, and told readers to “throw aside the fetter” when it became one.

    HRobert Murray M’Cheyne, 1842

  • Rules served the rhythm

    Benedict set out the weekly psalter in detail, then told anyone who found it unsatisfactory to arrange it better, as long as the whole psalter was prayed each week.

    HSaint Benedict, 6th century

  • Report truthfully

    The Cape Town Commitment calls for truthful reporting and questions demands for visible results. A progress screen is a report; it should not inflate or shame.

    SLausanne Movement

Motivation research

What counting does

  • Expected rewards can undermine interest

    Across 128 experiments, expected tangible rewards tied to doing a task reduced intrinsic interest; informational positive feedback did not.

    SDeci, Koestner and Ryan

  • Measuring can drain enjoyment

    In six experiments, measuring walking or reading made people do more but enjoy it less.

    RJordan Etkin

  • Gamification depends on design

    A meta-analysis of educational gamification finds effects that vary with design and context; points and badges are not a reliable lever on their own.

    SMichael Sailer and Lisa Homner

  • Autonomy matters

    Self-determination theory distinguishes doing something because one values it from doing it under pressure; support for autonomy, competence and relatedness sustains the first.

    SRyan and DeciSCenter for Self-Determination Theory

Habits and returning

Missing a day, starting again

  • Habits form at different speeds

    In a study of everyday habits, the time to automaticity varied widely between people, and missing a single opportunity did not materially set it back.

    SLally et al.

  • A broken streak makes stopping likelier

    Across seven studies, highlighting a broken streak reduced later engagement, most when people blamed themselves, and less when the streak could be repaired.

    RJackie Silverman and Alixandra Barasch

  • New beginnings help

    Goal-directed behaviour rises after temporal landmarks such as a new week or month: a natural moment to invite someone back.

    RHengchen Dai, Katherine L. Milkman and Jason Riis

  • Shame drives people away

    Shame focuses on the self and tends to prompt hiding and withdrawal; guilt about a specific act tends to prompt repair. A return screen should never be a verdict on the person.

    RJune Price Tangney, Jeff Stuewig and Debra J. Mashek

Where to start

Which fits your product?

  • IfSomeone is new to the app or to the Bible

    Start withA frame that starts with them: an activity chart from their first week, a short plan, or returns

  • IfSomeone chose a daily habit and wants feedback on it

    Start withA calendar or activity chart, with any streak optional and easy to hide

  • IfSomeone is working through a book or a finite plan

    Start withCoverage of that plan, not of the whole Bible

  • IfYour product leans on points and badges

    Start withMake them opt-in, and test what happens when someone loses a streak

  • IfYou don’t know yet

    Start withStart with no progress view and a saved place. Add a record when people ask for one

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What growth is

“I planted the seed and Apollos watered it, but God made it grow” (1 Corinthians 3:6). The seed grows, “though he knows not how” (Mark 4:27), and “the kingdom of God will not come with observable signs” (Luke 17:20). Paul pressed on, “forgetting what is behind” (Philippians 3:13–14). Spiritual growth is real, but it is mostly hidden, even from the person growing.

Where Christians differ

Some traditions value keeping a record: spiritual journals, Puritan diaries, Wesley’s accountability. Others warn that measuring piety breeds pride or despair. Willard argued that grace is opposed to earning, not to effort, and many readers find a simple record helpful for a chosen discipline.

What follows for design

A record of reading is not a measure of faith. Show what was read, not a score, let people turn tracking off, and never compare them with others. Leave room for the seasons when the best thing a person did was not open the app.

In conversation with

  • James K. A. Smith, You Are What You Love (2016) warns that “secular liturgies” carry a story about who we are; badges and leaderboards tell a story of competition.
  • Dallas Willard, “Spiritual Disciplines and Means of Grace” (2002) distinguished effort from earning: a record of reading must never become a record of merit.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Levels, leaderboards and badges turn reading into a contest.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Make any tracking optional and describe it honestly.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Show what was read rather than how much, and give up the engagement numbers a contest produces.

What the views leave out

Each view counts something an app can see. None can see a passage remembered on a hard day or a week spent caring for someone instead of opening the app. When would putting the phone down be the good outcome?

Expected, contingent rewards can weaken intrinsic interest under some conditions, while cues and habit support that someone chose can genuinely help. The research does not support a single rule such as “streaks help for a month and then backfire”.

Read the research note on gamification

Edge cases to design for

Define each state before designing the indicator. Store the saved passage separately from sessions the person marks complete and the dates assigned by a plan. Allow completion to be corrected. Rescheduling changes dates without filling gaps or erasing notes. Explain whether recorded activity is private or shared before enabling group visibility. Offer a clear next action; do not require a progress dashboard to reach the reading.

What tends to go wrong

Silently counting an opened passage as read; resetting completion when changing dates; presenting calendar delay as spiritual failure; using colour alone for status; turning a private activity record into a group ranking; claiming app activity captures reading elsewhere.

How to test it

Ask readers to find their next session, correct an accidental completion and join today’s group reading without completing earlier sessions. Repeat with a screen reader, large text and an offline update. Ask what the display implies about them and who can see it. Compare usefulness and pressure with a saved-place-only version.

Faith and interpretation

A record can serve a chosen discipline without becoming a verdict on the person. Daily reading may matter deeply to someone; a period of absence tells the app little about their faithfulness. Describe what is known and leave space for the person’s own account of its meaning.
