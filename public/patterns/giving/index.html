Giving and offerings

How should a faith app ask for gifts, and what should it never promise?

“Each one should give what he has decided in his heart to give, not out of regret or compulsion. For God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7). Giving is worship, and it pays for churches, missions and the apps people pray with. More of it now happens on a phone: in a church’s app, through a QR code during the service, or with a Give button in a Bible app. The design decides what is chosen for the giver, where the ask appears and what a gift is said to do. Most people who give through an app want it to be simple and safe. Design also for the person who is short of money this month, and for the one who has just told the app about their grief.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Start with no amount, and a one-off gift

    YouVersion’s give screen starts at zero with its monthly gift switched off; Churchome’s opens with $100.00 already filled in, and Abide’s donate sheet offers six amounts with $50 already chosen and a monthly box left unticked. An empty amount leaves the decision to the giver; if you suggest amounts, choose none in advance.

  2. 2

    Name who receives the gift, and what it pays for

    YouVersion asks whether a gift goes to the app or to Bible translation; Bible.is asks people to “Donate online” for recordings in oral cultures. Say which organisation receives the money, what it pays for, and whether a receipt follows.

  3. 3

    Keep the ask out of prayer, grief and crisis

    Giving belongs in a menu, after a service or on its own page, not between a prayer request and the next screen. Someone who has just written about their grief, or is in danger, should see help and nothing else.

    Sharing with a group
  4. 4

    Keep gifts and purchases apart

    Pray.com’s “Support & Go Ad Free” gives donors “an Ad Free Subscription”, and Glorify’s “Pay it Forward” ticks “Most popular” in advance. If a payment unlocks something, call it a price; if it is a gift, let it unlock nothing.

    First use
  5. 5

    Stopping is as easy as starting

    Keep every regular gift in one place, with its next date, and let it be changed or stopped there in one step. Churchome sends people to “your Pushpay account” to change one: say where, and link to it. Remind the giver before a yearly gift or a change in amount.

  6. 6

    Say what a gift does, truly, and when enough is enough

    YouVersion’s Sowers page sets Bibles per year beside a monthly amount; Pray as you go says 80% of a donation goes to its content. Give each figure a source and a date, and when a need is met, say so, as Moses did.

  7. 7

    Never promise a return

    A gift is not a seed that obliges God to send a harvest. Leave out anything that ties a gift to healing, prosperity, answered prayer or a blessing, and anything that ranks givers or shows who gave what.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Given freely, in secret, as one is able

  • Decided in the heart

    “Each one should give what he has decided in his heart to give, not out of regret or compulsion” (2 Corinthians 9:7); “the gift is acceptable according to what one has, not according to what he does not have” (2 Corinthians 8:12). The poor widow’s two coins were “more than all the others” (Mark 12:43).

  • In secret, and set aside

    “When you give to the needy, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your giving may be in secret” (Matthew 6:3–4). Paul asked each to “set aside a portion of his income” “on the first day of every week” (1 Corinthians 16:2): a regular gift, planned, not pressed out at the moment of asking.

  • Handled openly, and enough is enough

    Paul took “great care to do what is right, not only in the eyes of the Lord but also in the eyes of men” in handling a gift (2 Corinthians 8:21). When the people brought more than was needed for the tabernacle, which Bezalel built, Moses “sent a proclamation throughout the camp” and “the people were restrained from bringing more” (Exodus 36:6).

  • Tithe, offering and church tax

    Christians give in different ways: a tenth, as Israel brought “the full tithe into the storehouse” (Malachi 3:10); a proportion “as he is able” (Deuteronomy 16:17); weekly offerings; or, in Germany, church tax collected with income tax. Jesus did not set the tithe aside but named “justice, mercy and faithfulness” as weightier (Matthew 23:23).

Platforms and rules

What the stores, regulators and tax offices ask

  • A gift, or a purchase

    On iPhone, “Approved nonprofits may fundraise directly within their own apps”, with Apple Pay, and “must disclose how the funds will be used”; others “may only collect funds outside of the app”. A gift “connected to or associated at any point in time with receiving digital content or services must use in-app purchase”. Google Play exempts “tax exempt donations” from its billing.

    SAppleSGoogle Play Console Help

  • Pressure has a name

    The UK Code of Fundraising Practice requires “not putting undue pressure on a person to donate, not using unreasonably persistent approaches and accepting a request to end an interaction”. In 2026 US state regulators objected to “the application of a default “tip” that went directly to GoFundMe”.

    SFundraising Regulator (UK)SCrowell & Moring client alert, March 2026

  • Receipts belong to the gift

    In the US, organisations give a written acknowledgment for “contributions of $250 or more”, which may say that “the organization only provided intangible religious benefits”. A UK Gift Aid declaration must explain that “the donor is responsible to pay any difference”.

    SInternal Revenue Service (US)SHM Revenue & Customs (UK)

  • Current, complete and accurate

    Christian ministries in the ECFA agreed that what they tell donors “must be current, complete and accurate”, that past events “must be appropriately dated”, and that appeals “must not create unrealistic donor expectations of what a donor’s gift will actually accomplish”.

    SEvangelical Council for Financial Accountability

Research and practice

What defaults, recognition and promises do

  • A suggested amount moves people

    Goswami and Urminsky found “a scale-back effect, in which low defaults reduce average donation amounts”, and “a lower-bar effect, in which defaulting a low amount increases donation rate”. Any default also weakened “other cues such as positive charity information”.

    RIndranil Goswami and Oleg Urminsky

  • Being seen raises gifts

    In a field experiment, “charitable gifts increase in response to the promise of public recognition primarily because of individuals’ desire to improve their social image”: the motive Jesus warned against in Matthew 6.

    RDean Karlan and Margaret A. McConnell

  • Much church giving is now digital

    Churches that offer digital giving “reported that 41% of their giving came from digital methods”, and “Recurring giving accounted for 35% of the digital giving in 2025”, in one vendor’s US data. Givelify, the giving app with the most ratings (101K), says it “has processed $6 billion in donations”. In Württemberg almost 400 congregations take the collection through a QR code and a web form.

    SMinistry Brands, 2026SGivelifySDavid SeeleySWochenblatt-Reporter

  • Seeds and harvests

    Evangelical theologians at Lausanne described prosperity teaching that promises health and wealth through “the “sowing of seeds” through the faithful payments of tithes and offerings”, and warned against “Replacing the traditional call to repentance and faith with a call to give money”.

    TLausanne Theology Working Group, Africa chapter

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What giving is for

“Who am I, and who are my people, that we should be able to give as generously as this? For everything comes from You, and from Your own hand we have given to You” (1 Chronicles 29:14). Giving returns to God what was already his, supports those who serve the word, and cares for those in need. Jesus said that “It is more blessed to give than to receive” (Acts 20:35), and looked not at the size of a gift but at what it cost the giver.

Where Christians differ

Some churches teach the tithe as the measure of faithful giving; others teach proportional giving “as he is able” (Deuteronomy 16:17) and see the tithe as a starting point or as Israel’s law. Catholics give through the offertory, Mass offerings and parish appeals; in Germany church tax carries most church income and the collection is for a named cause. Pentecostal and charismatic churches range from careful teaching on stewardship to the prosperity teaching that the Lausanne statement rejected. All agree that a gift is free, and that those who receive it must handle it honestly.

What follows for design

Let people give what they decided in their heart: nothing chosen for them, nothing asked in a moment of need. Say who receives the gift and what it truly does, keep it in secret unless the giver chooses otherwise, and make a regular gift easy to stop. Never promise a return, and when the need is met, say so.

In conversation with

  • Henri Nouwen, A Spirituality of Fundraising (2010) wrote that “Fundraising is first and foremost a ministry”, “a way of announcing our vision and inviting other people into our mission”, and “precisely the opposite of begging”.
  • John Wesley, “The Use of Money” (sermon 50) told Methodists to “gain all you can”, “save all you can” and “give all you can”: giving is the end that earning and saving serve.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Choose the amount and the tier for the giver, switch on monthly by default, add a tip unless it is removed, ask after a prayer request, and promise a harvest for a seed.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Start with no amount and a one-off gift, name who receives it and what it pays for, show fees, send receipts, and let a regular gift be stopped in one step.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Ask only where people look for it, never in prayer, grief or need; give every figure a source and a date, say when enough has been given, and give up the gifts that pressure would bring.

Edge cases to design for

Put giving where someone looks for it, in a menu or after a service, not in the middle of reading or prayer. Start with an empty amount and a one-off gift; let the person switch on a regular gift and choose its day. Name who receives the gift and what it pays for, show fees as fees, send a receipt, and keep a history the person can stop or change in one place. Speak of what a gift does with current, complete and accurate figures, and say when enough has been given.

What tends to go wrong

A “Most popular” tier ticked in advance; a tip of a sixth of the gift added unless someone slides it to zero; a monthly gift switched on by default; a gift box on the screen after someone shares a prayer request; “Sow a seed of faith today and expect a harvest” beside a countdown; givers ranked by amount; a figure from five years ago presented as today’s; a recurring gift that can only be stopped by email.

How to test it

Give the smallest possible gift in your app as someone new, then as someone who has just lost their job, then as someone who has just shared a prayer request. Write down every amount, tick and toggle that was chosen for you, every place the ask appeared, and how many steps it takes to stop a regular gift. Then read every sentence about what a gift will do, and ask whether you could show the figure’s source and date.

Faith and interpretation

“Each one should give what he has decided in his heart to give, not out of regret or compulsion. For God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7). Jesus said that the poor widow who put in two small coins “has put more than all the others into the treasury” (Mark 12:43), and told those who give, “do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing” (Matthew 6:3). When the people brought more than enough for the tabernacle, Moses “sent a proclamation throughout the camp” to stop (Exodus 36:6).
