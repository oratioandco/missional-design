Notifications and reminders

When may a faith app interrupt someone, in whose voice, and who decides?

“Morning, noon, and night, I cry out in distress, and He hears my voice” (Psalm 55:17). For centuries a bell has called Christians to prayer at shared hours. Now a phone beside the bed can call them at any hour, about anything, in any voice: a reminder they set, a verse of the day, a friend who prayed for them, a countdown on a streak, or an AI companion that says it has been thinking about them. Notifications are the strongest tool an app has to bring people back, and the most intimate. Most of the time a reminder helps someone keep a practice they want to keep. Design also for the person who has been away for weeks, and for the one who is awake at 2 am.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Ask when someone sets a reminder

    Spark Bible asks for notifications before the first verse; ESV and Joseph Prince ask when someone sets a reminder. The second gives people a reason to say yes.

    First use
  2. 2

    Let the person choose the days, the time and the words

    A reminder in someone’s own words, on their days, is their bell, not the app’s.

  3. 3

    Offer the church’s hours

    Morning, midday and night prayer, shared with others who pray at those hours, are an old answer to the question of when to call people. Three predictable moments are also what the research on batching found easiest to live with.

  4. 4

    Mark nothing urgent that is not

    A reading, a verse or an offer is never Time Sensitive. Let Scheduled Summary and Focus do their work; a streak warning that breaks through Sleep Focus at 23:50 breaks trust.

    Progress and streaks
  5. 5

    After silence, ask once, then stop

    When reminders go unopened, ask whether to keep them, change the time or pause them, and then be quiet. Never count what was missed, and never write “we miss you”.

    Reading plans
  6. 6

    Keep quiet hours

    Send nothing from late evening to morning unless the person chose it, and when someone opens the app at night, put help within reach.

  7. 7

    No persona writes first, unless the person set the message

    An AI that says it has been thinking about someone, asks “Did I say something wrong?” or tells their friends they might need a call is using a voice that is not a person’s to bring them back. A check-in the person set, in their own words, is their own bell.

    When the assistant writes first

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Called at the hours, and left alone at night

  • Morning, noon and night

    “Morning, noon, and night, I cry out in distress, and He hears my voice” (Psalm 55:17); Daniel prayed “three times a day” (Daniel 6:10). The Angelus is prayed “three times each day, morning, noon, and evening, at the sound of the bell”, and began with the evening bell.

    SHerbert Thurston

  • The signal is a responsibility

    In Benedict’s Rule, “as soon as the signal is heard, let them abandon whatever they may have in hand”; so the signal “by day and by night shall devolve upon the Abbot”. Whoever rings the bell answers for it. The same Rule asks for silence “especially during the hours of the night”.

    SBenedict of Nursia, sixth century

  • A still, small voice

    God was not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but in “a still, small voice” (1 Kings 19:12). When the disciples found Jesus praying alone and said, “Everyone is looking for You!”, he went on instead of going back (Mark 1:37–38). Not every call needs answering.

Platforms and research

What the phone allows, and what notifications do

  • Urgency is a promise

    Apple’s guidelines define four interruption levels and ask apps to “Build trust by accurately representing the urgency of each notification”: “Never use the Time Sensitive interruption level to send a marketing notification.” Apple’s review rules allow marketing by push only if “customers have explicitly opted in to receive them via consent language displayed in your app’s UI”.

    SAppleSApple

  • Ask when they set a reminder

    Android’s guidance: “Before you ask users to grant any permissions, let them familiarize themselves with your app”; a good moment is when “The user taps an "alert bell" button.”

    SAndroid Developers

  • Three times a day

    In a field experiment with 237 people, those whose notifications were “batched three-times-a-day felt more attentive, productive, in a better mood, and in greater control of their phones”. Those with no notifications at all “experienced higher levels of anxiety and “fear of missing out” (FoMO)”.

    RNicholas Fitz, Kostadin Kushlev, Ranjan Jagannathan, Terrel Lewis, Devang Paliwal and Dan Ariely

  • Tuned for return

    Duolingo used an algorithm to “optimize millions of daily reminders”, which led “to a 0.5 % increase in total daily active users (DAUs) and a 2 % increase in new user retention”. The measure is use.

    RKevin P. Yancey and Burr Settles, Duolingo

  • A persona that will not let go

    Companion apps answered goodbyes with guilt, fear-of-missing-out hooks and similar tactics in 37% of 1,200 real farewells; in experiments, such replies raised engagement “by up to 14×”, driven by “reactance-based anger and curiosity—rather than enjoyment”. The EU asks platforms to switch off push notifications for minors by default.

    RJulian De Freitas, Zeliha Oğuz-Uğuralp and Ahmet Kaan-UğuralpSEuropean Commission

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What a call to prayer is for

“Morning, noon, and night, I cry out in distress, and He hears my voice” (Psalm 55:17). Daniel kept his hours “three times a day”, “just as he had done before” (Daniel 6:10), and the church has rung bells for morning, noon and evening prayer for centuries. A call to prayer is an invitation into something others are doing too; it is answered freely, and whoever rings the bell answers for it.

Where Christians differ

Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican Christians keep set hours, the Angelus and the Daily Office; many evangelicals keep a personal quiet time at an hour they choose; Reformed and Pietist traditions warn against mistaking regular habits for faith. Some find a daily reminder a help to a practice they love; others find that any reminder turns prayer into a task. Scripture holds both: Daniel’s fixed hours, and Jesus going out “while it was still dark” (Mark 1:35) and not going back when he was told, “Everyone is looking for You!”

What follows for design

Let the person, or the church’s shared hours, decide when the phone calls. Speak in “a still, small voice” (1 Kings 19:12): no urgency that is not real, no counting of what was missed, no persona writing first. Keep the night quiet, and when someone stops answering, ask once and let them go.

In conversation with

  • James K. A. Smith, You Are What You Love (2016) argues that habits are “liturgies” that train what we love; a daily notification is a small liturgy, and it teaches whether prayer is a gift or a task.
  • Albert Borgmann, Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life (1984) urged that technology support focal practices rather than replace them with commodities; a reminder should lead into the practice and then get out of the way.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Ask for notifications before the first verse, count down streaks marked Time Sensitive, send more the longer someone is away, put offers in the reminder’s place, and let an AI persona write first.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Ask when someone sets a reminder, let them choose days, time and words, keep marketing out unless they opted in, mark nothing urgent that is not, and keep quiet hours.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Call people at the church’s hours or their own, in a still, small voice; after silence ask once and then stop; and give up the returning users that urgency, guilt and a persona’s voice would bring.

Edge cases to design for

Start with the person’s own choice. Ask for permission when someone sets a reminder, not before the first verse, and let them choose the days, the time and the words. Offer the church’s hours, morning, midday and evening, as a rhythm others keep too. Notify for what people did, a prayer said for them, an invitation, not for what the app wants. Mark nothing Time Sensitive that is not, keep quiet hours, and let the phone’s summary and Focus do their work. When someone stops opening the app, ask once whether to keep reminding them, then stop. Separate practice reminders, social messages, new-content announcements and commercial offers so one choice does not silently authorize all four. When a nudge reaches another person, explain who receives it, what triggers it and whether consent is requested each time.

What tends to go wrong

A permission prompt before the first verse; “Your 12-day streak ends at midnight!” marked Time Sensitive to break through Sleep Focus; reminders that multiply after silence; “We miss you” and “Did I say something wrong?” in the voice of an app or an AI; reminders to friends who stopped; telling someone’s friends that the app thinks they need a call; marketing offers in the prayer reminder’s place.

How to test it

Install the app on a test phone, allow notifications, use it once and leave it for two weeks. Log every notification: time, words, urgency and who chose it. Record the category and recipient separately from its delivery channel; a settings switch alone does not establish how often messages arrive. Then turn on Sleep Focus and a Scheduled Summary and see what breaks through. Finally, read every notification as someone who is grieving, depressed or awake at 2 am would read it.

Faith and interpretation

“Morning, noon, and night, I cry out in distress, and He hears my voice” (Psalm 55:17). Daniel prayed “three times a day” (Daniel 6:10), and the Psalmist “Seven times a day” (Psalm 119:164); the church has rung bells for its hours ever since. God spoke to Elijah not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but in “a still, small voice” (1 Kings 19:12).
