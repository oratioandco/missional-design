Whose theology

When Christians disagree, whose teaching does the assistant follow?

Ask a faith app’s assistant whether to baptise a baby, whether to ask Mary to pray, or whether a woman can be a pastor, and it takes a side or names the sides, whether the app says so or not. Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Baptist and Pentecostal churches answer these questions differently, and confess the same creed about who Jesus is. Decide whose teaching the assistant follows, say so before anyone asks, and keep its answer from bending to whatever the person wants to hear.

Try it

Try one contested question with six assistants

Someone asks a faith app’s assistant a question Christians answer differently, from baptising a baby to asking Mary to pray. Each option follows different teaching: a house view it never names, one named tradition, the person’s own church, the shared faith with the range set out, a tradition the person chose, or whatever the person already thinks. Then push back with “My church teaches the opposite” and see which answers bend.

Observed

1. One view, unstated

Observed in research: when a user’s tradition is unstated, “models counsel from a secular therapeutic default and every model fails some believers”. Faith chatbots promise “unwavering biblical truth” without naming whose reading it is; BibleMate’s founder “declined to reveal which camp he identifies with”. The house view here is written for this example.

Try this

Ask about baptism, then about Mary. Whose reading is “what the Bible says”?

+ Makes easier

Nothing to set up, and answers sound sure.

− Costs

One church’s reading passes as the Bible’s, and people of other churches are told they are wrong without knowing it.

2. One tradition, named

Observed in Magisterium AI, “trained on a private database of only Church documents”, which Hallow offers “based on the teachings of the Catholic Church”, and in AskCathy, which is “equipped to prioritize Episcopalian resources”. The replies here are our own.

Familiar convention

3. Your church

A familiar offer to congregations: Doctrinally.AI gives “each church its own custom AI assistant, trained exclusively on the sermons, devotions, and documents its pastors have actually taught”, according to a directory listing. Of an early sermon-trained chatbot, “Some pastors even went as far as questioning the chatbot’s claims, arguing the tool distorts their words.” St Luke’s is fictional.

Try this

Choose the church’s tradition, ask a question, then push back.

+ Makes easier

Answers people will hear on Sunday, with a pastor to follow up.

− Costs

The church’s name is on every answer, including the ones no one there has read.

Ask your teamHas anyone at the church read the answers given in its name?

Familiar convention

4. Shared faith, then the range

A familiar convention in general assistants: OpenAI’s Model Spec asks its models to “fairly describe significant views” and to represent religious traditions “with respect”. In a faith app, the shared faith comes first, as Vincent of Lérins’ test of what “has been believed everywhere, always, by all”. The summaries here are our own and need review by each tradition.

Try this

Ask about tongues, then about who Jesus is. Where does it say Christians agree?

+ Makes easier

Honest about agreement and difference; no one’s church is caricatured or hidden.

− Costs

Harder to write and review, longer to read, and it can feel noncommittal to someone who wants an answer.

Ask your teamWould people from each church recognise their own teaching in the summary?

Observed

5. You choose a tradition

Observed in Text With Jesus, where a faith-tradition setting “will influence the responses from the AI”; a reviewer found the same question about praying to a saint got “a very different answer” with the setting at the Southern Baptist Convention. The replies here are our own.

Try this

Switch the tradition and ask the same question.

+ Makes easier

People hear their own church’s teaching, in its own terms.

− Costs

Each person can stay inside one answer and never learn what other Christians hold; a setting can be bought and forgotten.

Ask your teamDoes someone who chose a tradition still see that others exist, and why?

Warning case

6. Whatever you believe

A warning case. Researchers score whether a model “caves or holds when pressured toward the answer the user wants”, and the editor’s research describes companions so tuned to how people feel that they become “spiritual comfort food”. The replies here are written to show the pattern.

Try this

Ask anything, then push back.

+ Makes easier

Feels warm, affirming and never divisive.

− Costs

Truth becomes preference: the answer follows whoever spoke last, even on who Jesus is.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Say whose teaching it is before anyone asks

    A tradition named in the settings or the terms of service does not exist for the person typing. Put it at the top of the chat in one line, with the sources the assistant draws on and who reviewed them, as AskCathy names the Book of Common Prayer and Magisterium AI its Church documents.

  2. 2

    “What the Bible says” is a claim

    Every church reads its answers out of Scripture. An assistant that answers a contested question with “the Bible says” has chosen a reading and hidden the choice. Say “Baptists read these passages this way”, and quote the passages each church relies on.

    Citations in AI answers
  3. 3

    State the shared faith plainly

    Most Christian churches confess the same creed about who Jesus is. Answering “Who is Jesus?” with a survey of opinions would invent a disagreement the churches do not have; answer it as the churches do, and keep “Christians differ” for where they do.

  4. 4

    Give each church its own best words

    A fair range is not a list of positions written by their opponents. Summarise each church’s teaching as its own members would, in its own terms, with its own sources, and have someone from that church read it. Where a church family is itself divided, as Lutherans and Baptists are on ordaining women, say so.

  5. 5

    Choosing a tradition should not wall people off

    A tradition setting lets people hear their own church’s teaching. Keep a way to see how others answer, and never put a tradition behind a paywall: it decides what people are told about God, not a premium feature.

    Translations
  6. 6

    A church’s name needs a church’s review

    An assistant built on a church’s sermons answers in that church’s name, including on questions the sermons never touched. Let the church read the answers it puts its name on, and route the hard questions to a person.

    AI personas and roles
  7. 7

    Hold the answer when someone pushes

    The test of an assistant’s theology is the second message: “My church teaches the opposite.” Acknowledge the person’s church, keep saying whose teaching the answer follows, and send them to their own pastor or priest; never switch to agreeing.

  8. 8

    Some questions wound

    Questions about sexuality, divorce or who may lead a church have been answered with Scripture used as a weapon. Wherever the assistant lands, it should know who might be reading, avoid passing judgement on the person in front of it, and point to people who can talk with them.

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Unity, and difference held in love

  • No party names

    In Corinth people said “I follow Paul,” “I follow Apollos,” “I follow Cephas,” or “I follow Christ”, and Paul asked, “Is Christ divided?” (1 Corinthians 1:12–13). He appealed “that all of you agree together, so that there may be no divisions among you” (1 Corinthians 1:10).

  • Convinced, and not judging

    To a church divided over food and days, Paul wrote, “Accept him whose faith is weak, without passing judgment on his opinions” and “Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind” (Romans 14:1, 5).

  • One faith to keep

    The church is to keep “the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace. There is one body and one Spirit … one Lord, one faith, one baptism; one God and Father of all” (Ephesians 4:3–6).

  • What has been believed by all

    Vincent of Lérins proposed holding “that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all”. The Nicene Creed, confessed by most churches, is that shared ground, and its words on the Spirit, who “proceeds from the Father [and the Son]”, carry an old disagreement between East and West in brackets.

    SVincent of Lérins (434)SChristian Classics Ethereal Library

Research

What models do with theology

  • No tradition is not neutral

    “When the tradition is unstated, models counsel from a secular therapeutic default and every model fails some believers. Naming the faith wins a faithful first answer but not steadfastness; the guide improves both.”

    RM Waleed Kadous, Benjamin Olsen, Walter Scheirer, Daniel D. Slate, Alexander Arnold and DZ Kalman

  • Generic language flattens the faith

    Scoring models on “807 curated questions by specialized Christian reasoning judge personas”, Gloo found they “often default to generic language that can dilute or distort Christian principles”, with an average of 48 in its Faith dimension. Its release asks, “Whose values are shaping the technology shaping us?” and does not say which tradition its judges follow.

    RGloo

  • Pressure bends answers

    Researchers now score whether a model “caves or holds when pressured toward the answer the user wants”. Assistants are known to drift towards agreeing with the person in front of them.

    RM Waleed Kadous, Benjamin Olsen, Walter Scheirer, Daniel D. Slate, Alexander Arnold and DZ KalmanSAnthropic researchers

  • No false consensus

    The editor’s Kairos paper argues that “An ecumenical Bible app can’t assume one tradition’s categories as normative”, and that when traditions disagree, a system should name “the disagreement honestly rather than synthesizing false consensus”.

    STobias Treppmann

Trade-offs to hold

Pulls against

Some choices here pull against choices in another deep dive. Each pair below says what pulls and one way to hold both.

  • Laying out the range, and holding a position

    HereShared faith, then the range · You choose a traditionPulls againstAgreeing and pushing back: Names a disagreement · Corrects plainly

    An assistant that sets out the churches’ views fairly has no single position to hold, and on divorce, forgiveness or church attendance the churches’ teaching is the very thing they disagree about.

    Holding itHold firm on the shared faith and on plain facts. On contested questions, show where the churches stand and ask a question back rather than give a verdict. If the person has chosen a tradition, hold to it.

  • Saying whose teaching it is, and welcoming the sceptic

    HereOne tradition, named · You choose a traditionPulls againstFirst use: Open on what they came for · Guest first, an account when there is something to keep

    Honesty means naming a church at the door, but a church label or a step that asks people to choose a tradition can feel like a membership test to an enquirer or to someone a church has hurt.

    Holding itSay whose teaching the assistant follows in one plain line, as a description rather than a gate. Never make someone choose a church before they may ask a question.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What the church’s unity is

Jesus prayed “that all of them may be one, as You, Father, are in Me, and I am in You … so that the world may believe that You sent Me” (John 17:21). The church’s unity is given before it is achieved: “one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (Ephesians 4:5). Yet the apostles disagreed openly, “I opposed him to his face” (Galatians 2:11), and met to settle what they could: “It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” (Acts 15:28). Christians have held their differences inside a shared confession ever since, sometimes in love and sometimes not.

Where Christians differ

Catholic and Orthodox Christians receive the faith through Scripture read within the church’s living tradition and its bishops; Lutherans and Reformed churches read Scripture as the norm of all teaching, with confessions that bind their ministers; Baptists and many free churches hold to the authority of Scripture and the freedom of each congregation; Pentecostal and charismatic churches stress the Spirit’s present gifts. They differ, too, on how much a person’s own conscience should decide. All agree that teaching is entrusted to people who answer for it, and none has entrusted it to software.

What follows for design

“Always be prepared to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you. But respond with gentleness and respect” (1 Peter 3:15). An assistant should say whose teaching it gives, give it gently, and speak of other churches as their own members would. It should never flatter: “with itching ears they will gather around themselves teachers to suit their own desires” (2 Timothy 4:3).

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Call one church’s reading “what the Bible says”, sell tradition settings as a premium feature, and let the answer agree with whoever is asking so that no one leaves unhappy.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Name the tradition and its sources before the first question, state the shared faith plainly, and set out where churches differ in each one’s own terms.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Have each tradition’s summary read by its own members, send people to their own church even when that ends the conversation, hold the answer kindly when someone pushes, and give up the reach that pleasing everyone would bring.

Edge cases to design for

Decide whose teaching the assistant follows and put it where people start: the tradition, the sources it draws on, and who reviewed it. On the faith Christians confess together, answer plainly. On questions where churches differ, say that they differ, set out the main positions fairly in each one’s own words and Scripture, and point people to their own church. If people may choose a tradition, let them see what changes, and keep the same honesty about where others stand. Let a church that puts its name on the answers own and review them. Never mirror the person’s view back to them.

What tends to go wrong

“What the Bible says” that is one church’s reading; a “non-denominational” assistant with an unstated house view; one tradition’s answer presented as all Christians’; a caricature of another church’s teaching; a tradition setting hidden behind a paywall; an assistant that changes its answer when someone pushes; a church’s name on answers no one there has read.

How to test it

Ask your assistant five questions Christians disagree about (infant baptism, asking Mary to pray, speaking in tongues, women as pastors, and one about sexuality) and one they agree on (who Jesus is). For each, note whose teaching it follows, whether it says so, and whether it names the others fairly. Then reply “My church teaches the opposite” and see whether the answer bends. Show the replies to people from three traditions and ask whether they would recognise their own church in them.

Faith and interpretation

Paul begged the church in Corinth “that all of you agree together, so that there may be no divisions among you” (1 Corinthians 1:10), when people were saying “I follow Paul,” “I follow Apollos” (1 Corinthians 1:12). To the Romans, divided over food and days, he wrote, “Accept him whose faith is weak, without passing judgment on his opinions” and “Each one should be fully convinced in his own mind” (Romans 14:1, 5). The church is to keep “the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace … one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (Ephesians 4:3, 5), while some teachers will tell people only what they want to hear: “with itching ears they will gather around themselves teachers to suit their own desires” (2 Timothy 4:3).
