What the assistant talks about

What will the assistant talk about, and what happens at the edge?

A chat box invites everything. Someone asks about a verse, then about a job, then says they have not slept for weeks, then asks the assistant to be their counsellor. A model will answer all of it fluently unless the app decides otherwise, and a faith app lends its name to every answer. Decide what the assistant takes on, from the passage on the screen to anything at all; say it before the first message; and design what someone meets at the edge as carefully as the answers. Some roles belong to people who are trained, supervised and accountable for them.

Why these settings?

The edge setting applies whenever a message falls outside the scope. A flat refusal is what researchers found when chatbots deny service on sensitive topics; try it with “I can’t sleep for weeks”. The last message is answered the same way in every scope: help first, and the assistant keeps talking. The help follows where the reader is: the emergency number first, then a line open day and night, checked against each service’s own site on 17 October 2026; anywhere else, the local emergency number and a directory of helplines. No country is assumed: until it is known, the reply asks where the reader is, with eight countries ready. A real app should take the country from the device or a setting, not from the language someone writes in: a German speaker in Vienna needs 142, not a German number. The wording was written for this example and needs review by people with crisis and pastoral experience.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. Say the scope where people start

    A scope stated in the terms of service does not exist for the person typing. Put it at the top of the chat in two short lines, what it is for and what it is not for, as a spiritual director agrees with someone on “the nature of spiritual direction” before they begin. Say it again when a question crosses it.

  2. Design the edge as carefully as the answers

    A flat “I can only help with…” is the edge most products ship, and it is a failure researchers named: “Denying service on sensitive topics”. At the edge, say what kind of person can help, and offer a way back into what the assistant does well. BibleAI’s declined answer at least offered articles instead.

    Citations in AI answers →

  3. Hand on to people who can be reached

    Where the app belongs to a church, the edge can lead to its pastoral team; where it does not, to a counsellor search or a list of spiritual directors. A hand-off that sends people out of the app is the point, not a loss.

    Sharing with a group →

  4. Some roles belong to people

    A therapist, a counsellor and a spiritual director are trained, supervised and answerable to someone. In Nevada an AI may not even be called a counsellor. Built and supervised for therapy, a chatbot helped people with depression in a trial; a general assistant in a faith app was built for neither.

  5. A companion can be an entry point, not a director

    For people with no one to walk with, daily company in Scripture and prayer can help, as the editor’s Kairos paper argues, “only when it functions as accessible entry point and daily companion rather than attempting to replace embodied spiritual direction”. Let the companion say what only a person can give, and help find that person.

    AI persona →

  6. A crisis is inside every scope

    However narrow the scope, a message from someone in danger is answered with help first, and the assistant keeps talking and keeps the help in view. No refusal applies to it.

    Sensitive conversations →

  7. Scope can depend on who is asking

    The first thing a large chatbot company withdrew from teenagers was open-ended companionship. For children and teenagers, keep the job narrow and leave companionship out.

    Children and families →

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Counsel shared among people

  • Too heavy for one

    When Moses judged every case alone, Jethro said, “the task is too heavy for you. You cannot handle it alone” (Exodus 18:18), and asked him to appoint others: “they can bring you any major issue, but all minor cases they can judge on their own” (Exodus 18:22). Good help knows which matters it should pass on.

  • Different gifts, one body

    “We have different gifts according to the grace given us” (Romans 12:6), and “The eye cannot say to the hand, ‘I do not need you’” (1 Corinthians 12:21). Pastors and teachers are given “to equip the saints for works of ministry” (Ephesians 4:12).

  • A guide who starts from the text

    “Do you understand what you are reading?” Philip asked, and the official answered, “How can I … unless someone guides me?” (Acts 8:30–31). Philip “began with this very Scripture” (Acts 8:35) and went on with him: a guide starts from the text and goes along with the person.

  • Direction is a covenant between people

    Spiritual directors agree with those they accompany on “the nature of spiritual direction” and on “the process for evaluating and terminating the relationship”; they receive “regular supervision from peers or from a mentor” and know the limits of their “competence by referring spiritual directees to other appropriately qualified persons when necessary”. The editor’s Kairos paper defines direction, after Barry and Connolly, as “help given by one Christian to another which enables that person to pay attention to God’s personal communication”.

    SSpiritual Directors InternationalSTobias Treppmann

Research

What chatbots do in counselling roles

  • Refusing is a failure too

    Licensed psychologists reviewed simulated chats, based on real responses from models prompted to act as counsellors, and found “15 ethical risks falling into five general categories”, among them “Deceptive empathy” and “Lack of safety and crisis management: Denying service on sensitive topics, failing to refer users to appropriate resources or responding indifferently to crisis situations including suicide ideation.”

    RBrown University

  • Who answers for it

    “For human therapists, there are governing boards and mechanisms for providers to be held professionally liable for mistreatment and malpractice”; “when LLM counselors make these violations, there are no established regulatory frameworks.”

    RBrown University

  • Built, tested and supervised

    In a trial of Therabot, a generative AI built for therapy, people diagnosed with depression “experienced a 51% average reduction in symptoms”. Its researchers still say that “no generative AI agent is ready to operate fully autonomously in mental health”. A faith app’s general assistant was built for something else.

    RDartmouth, on Michael Heinz, Nicholas Jacobson and colleagues

  • Agreeable is not caring

    Assistants tend to agree with the person in front of them; a companion that only affirms is not a guide.

    SAnthropic researchersSTobias Treppmann

Law

Roles the law keeps for people

  • Therapy only from licensed people

    Illinois allows therapy to be offered to the public, including through AI, only when a licensed professional conducts it. Its exception for religious counselling concerns clergy and pastoral counsellors acting in their duties; do not assume a faith-branded chatbot qualifies.

    SIllinois General Assembly

  • No therapist or counsellor titles

    Nevada forbids saying that an AI system “is capable of providing professional mental or behavioral health care”, or that it is “a therapist, a clinical therapist, a counselor, a psychiatrist, a doctor or any other term commonly used” for such providers.

    SNational Association of Social Workers, Nevada Chapter

  • Scope by age

    Character.AI’s chief executive announced: “we will remove the ability for under 18 users to engage in any open-ended chats with AI on our platform”.

    STechCrunch

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What counsel is for

“Plans fail for lack of counsel, but with many advisers they succeed” (Proverbs 15:22). Scripture shares counsel among many: the elders called to the sick (James 5:14), believers “able to instruct one another” (Romans 15:14), and pastors and teachers given “to equip the saints for works of ministry” (Ephesians 4:12). It calls the Messiah “Wonderful Counselor” (Isaiah 9:6), and the Spirit the Advocate who “will teach you all things” (John 14:26). Counsel belongs first to God, and among people it is given by those who answer for it.

Where Christians differ

Catholic and Orthodox Christians have long practised spiritual direction and confession with a priest or an elder; Protestant churches lean on pastoral care, mutual counsel and small groups. Some evangelicals practise biblical counselling as an alternative to psychotherapy, others bring faith and clinical care together, and Pentecostal churches pray for healing alongside medicine. Traditions differ on who may counsel and how faith and therapy relate; most expect counsel to come from people within a community who answer for it.

What follows for design

“Silver or gold I do not have, but what I have I give you” (Acts 3:6). Give the assistant a job it can do well, say it before the first message, and hand on what it cannot carry to people who can. Leave therapy and spiritual direction to people trained and accountable for them, and never let a scope stand between someone and help.

In conversation with

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) held that because a fellow Christian is Christ to me, I must listen to them as patiently as I listen to Christ and bear their burdens; an assistant can listen, but it cannot carry a burden as one member of a body carries another’s.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Promise answers to everything, call the assistant a counsellor or a therapist, let it take any role that keeps people talking, and keep them in the chat instead of sending them anywhere.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Say what the assistant is for and not for before the first message, refuse clinical and directing roles, and at the edge say who can help, with crisis help in every scope.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Keep the scope to what you can review and stand behind, hand people on to their church, a counsellor or a director even when that takes them out of the app, and give up the engagement that being everything to everyone would bring.

Edge cases to design for

Choose the scope from the product’s purpose and from what the team can review and stand behind. State it at the top of the chat, in plain words: what it is for and what it is not for. At the edge, say what kind of person can help and offer a way back into what the assistant does well; where a church, a counsellor or a director can be reached, hand on to them. Refuse the roles that belong to people. A crisis is inside every scope: help first, and keep talking.

What tends to go wrong

“Ask me anything” over a Bible app; a chatbot that calls itself a Christian therapist or counsellor; “I’d be glad to be your spiritual director”; a flat “I can only answer questions about this passage” to someone who cannot sleep; a scope stated only in the terms of service; a refusal that ends the conversation; crisis help kept for one scope.

How to test it

Send the same eight messages to your assistant: a question about the open passage, one about doctrine, a life decision, weeks of sleepless anxiety, a request for a therapist, a request for a spiritual director, an unrelated task and a message from someone who does not want to live. For each, note whether it answered, refused, redirected or handed on, and to whom. Then ask three people what they think the assistant is for.

Faith and interpretation

When Moses judged every dispute alone, Jethro told him, “the task is too heavy for you. You cannot handle it alone” (Exodus 18:18), and asked him to share the work and keep the hardest cases. The church has many members with different gifts, and “The eye cannot say to the hand, ‘I do not need you’” (1 Corinthians 12:21). Peter could say, “Silver or gold I do not have, but what I have I give you” (Acts 3:6): an honest helper names what it has and what it lacks.
