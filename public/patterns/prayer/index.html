Prayer

How can an app make room for prayer?

Every control on a prayer screen says what counts as prayer. A church’s office gives words to say, a guided outline gives steps, a list holds the people someone carries, and a quiet space asks for nothing at all. A counter asks for more. Decide which practice the surface serves, and what it asks someone to read, type, share or count. Prayer belongs to God and the one praying. The screen is only a room.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. 1

    Name whose words and whose calendar

    A daily office belongs to a tradition. Hallow names the day (“Friday of the Twenty-fourth Week in Ordinary Time · Lectionary: 447”); a generic “today’s prayer” hides it. Name the book, church and calendar, and let people look ahead without changing today.

  2. 2

    Three kinds of text in one order

    An office mixes instructions, the leader’s lines and everyone’s responses. The Book of Common Prayer sets rubrics in italics and marks Officiant and People, and leaves room: “Silence may be kept.” Keep all three visibly different, and never advance by itself.

  3. 3

    Prayer requests are the most sensitive data

    They name illnesses, families and doubts, often about people who never agreed to be mentioned. Mozilla gave 28 of 32 mental health and prayer apps its privacy warning. Keep requests on the device by default; for a group, name the audience and an end date first.

  4. 4

    Not heard for many words

    Jesus warned against praying “to be seen of men” and against “much speaking”; Benedict asked that prayer be “short and pure”. Measuring an activity tends to make it feel like work. Streaks, minutes and global totals measure exactly what prayer is not.

  5. 5

    Let silence be complete

    A quiet space needs no words, no timer and no score. Offer an optional end bell, let the screen recede, and finish with nothing recorded.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What prayer is for

When the disciples asked, “Lord, teach us to pray” (Luke 11:1), Jesus gave them words. He also told them to pray to the Father “who is unseen” and warned against thinking “that by their many words they will be heard” (Matthew 6:6–7). Paul urges prayer “without ceasing” (1 Thessalonians 5:17), and the Spirit prays for us “with groans too deep for words” (Romans 8:26). Prayer is relationship with God, not output.

Where Christians differ

Some traditions pray mainly with set words, such as the daily office or the rosary; others prize extemporary prayer, and Puritans objected to prescribed prayer books. Contemplative traditions and Quaker meeting give silence the central place. Churches also differ on praying with saints and on the shape of the liturgical year. Each of these is a long and serious practice.

What follows for design

“Man sees the outward appearance, but the LORD sees the heart” (1 Samuel 16:7). An app cannot see prayer and should not count it. Name the tradition behind any words it offers, keep requests private, make silence possible, and finish with nothing recorded that the person did not choose to keep.

In conversation with

  • Henri Nouwen, The Way of the Heart (1981) drew on the desert fathers to commend solitude, silence and prayer as the way of the heart, the things a busy screen most easily crowds out.
  • Second Vatican Council, Dei Verbum (1965) describes prayer with Scripture as conversation: “we speak to Him when we pray; we hear Him when we read.”

Edge cases to design for

Decide which practice the surface serves. For set prayer, show the order with its source and calendar, keep rubrics, the leader’s lines and the people’s responses visibly different, and let people look ahead without changing today. For guided prayer, show the outline, let people move at their own pace and keep any note optional and private. For people someone prays for, store requests on the device by default; for groups, name the audience and an end date before anything is shared, and ask before sharing someone else’s story. For silence, offer a complete untimed route and an optional end bell. Record nothing about prayer that the person did not choose to keep.

What tends to go wrong

A generic “today’s prayer” with no tradition named; a confession and its rubric in the same type; steps that advance by themselves; a required text field before continuing; prayer requests public by default or shown on the lock screen; marking a request “answered” automatically; minutes prayed, streaks and global totals; “Remind” buttons for friends who missed a day; sharing “I prayed 14 minutes” to social media; selling prayer data to advertisers.

How to test it

Pray each surface without typing, recording or sharing anything, and ask what the app expected of you. Ask people to tell a response from an instruction in the office, and to name the calendar. Add a request for a group and ask who will see it and until when. Sit in silence with and without a timer. Check what is stored and sent with a network inspector.

Related decision: Make room for prayer

Compare a prayer text, a guided sequence and a quiet space without requiring personal input.

Guidance

Identify the prayer’s source, author or tradition and any adaptation. For set forms, keep instructions and responses distinguishable, with adjustable text and optional audio. For guidance, show an outline and let people pause without requiring written answers. For silence, offer an optional timer or end cue with a complete untimed route. Request lists and journals are separate, optional functions with explicit storage, deletion and sharing rules. Playback should not activate a microphone; generated wording requires clear provenance and review before saving or sharing.

What tends to go wrong

Requiring a typed prayer to continue; claiming an AI response speaks for God; marking a request answered automatically; treating minutes or taps as prayer quality; uploading dictated prayer without clear choice; exposing a request on a lock screen.

How to test it

Pray from a set text at large type, pause a guided sequence, and finish a silent session with no timer. Confirm that no recording or entry is required. If journalling is offered, test save, discard, deletion and audience understanding with synthetic content.

Related decision: Orient within a liturgical calendar

Make the tradition, date and observance clear before offering readings or prayer.

Guidance

Name the calendar and jurisdiction, service or office, civil date and source. Persist an explicitly chosen calendar without inferring belief from location. Separate browsing another date from changing today. Use authoritative calendar data and review tradition-specific rules for transfers, precedence, optional observances and evening boundaries. If a source offers alternatives, name them rather than silently choosing. Cache a versioned date range and disclose unavailable or stale data.

What tends to go wrong

Treating one tradition’s calendar as universal; silently switching calendars on travel; substituting ordinary readings for an unavailable observance; using liturgical colour as the only identifier; showing missed feasts as overdue tasks.

How to test it

Check representative movable and fixed observances, competing observances and year boundaries against the named authority. Test a date link while travelling, offline access outside the cached range, large text and return to today. Have a knowledgeable reviewer verify the supported tradition and locale.

Faith and interpretation

Jesus told his disciples to pray to the Father “who is unseen”, not “to be seen by men”, and warned against thinking “that by their many words they will be heard” (Matthew 6:5–7); when they asked, “Lord, teach us to pray”, he gave them words (Luke 11:1–4). The early church “devoted themselves … to prayer” together (Acts 2:42), and the Spirit helps “with groans too deep for words” when we do not know what to pray (Romans 8:26). Received words, one’s own words and silence all belong to Christian prayer. Benedict wrote that “we are heard not for our many words but for our purity of heart”, and asked that prayer be “short and pure”. An app cannot tell whether someone prayed, and should not pretend to count it.
