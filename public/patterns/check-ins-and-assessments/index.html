Check-ins and assessments

What may an app ask about someone, and what may it conclude?

Christians have asked themselves questions for centuries, in the evening Examen, in Wesley’s band meetings and in church gift inventories. Apps now ask too: setup quizzes, daily mood check-ins, long personality tests. Asking can serve personalisation, self-knowledge or a church listening to its members. Decide what each question is for, what is kept, who sees the result, and how much the result claims to know. Being asked about one’s soul is an act of trust, and the answers deserve the care of a pastor, not the appetite of a funnel.

Design considerations

Takeaways

  1. Say what each answer changes

    Abide asks “What brings you here today?” with twelve goals, and Bible BFF asks “How much of the Bible have you read?”, with testimonials and a paywall between the steps. A setup question earns its place when it changes what the app offers, says so, and can be skipped.

  2. Naming a feeling is enough

    Soultime’s check-in asks how you are “With Myself”, “With Others” and “With God”, an echo of the Examen’s attention to feelings. Labelling an emotion can itself ease it. Respond with a passage or a next step, not a chart to keep improving.

  3. Moods and faith are protected data

    Answers about anxiety, shame or God are health and religious data under GDPR Article 9, and inferences count too. Keep them on the device by default, let people delete them, and never send anything to friends or leaders automatically, even on a hard day.

  4. A result is a starting point, not a verdict

    Gift inventories overlap heavily with personality, and generic reports feel accurate to almost everyone. Willow Creek’s Network paired the inventory with a consultant and a place to serve. Say how a result was made, and point to trying things and to people who know the person.

  5. Churches have measured for decades

    Natural Church Development surveys 30 members and reports eight areas; Reveal surveyed whole congregations about spiritual growth, and taught its own church something uncomfortable. Anonymous, aggregated surveys can help a church listen. Scores, stages and ranks for individuals measure what only God sees.

Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What self-examination is for

“Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith” (2 Corinthians 13:5); “Search me, O God, and know my heart” (Psalm 139:23). Self-examination in Scripture is done before God, often with others, and it leads to confession and change, not to a profile. The Examen and Wesley’s band questions kept that shape.

Where Christians differ

Evangelical churches have embraced gifts inventories and church health surveys as tools for service and mission. Others are wary of importing management measurement into the church, as Mark Galli asked of Willow Creek’s Reveal. Traditions also differ on the gifts themselves: some expect every gift listed in the New Testament today, others hold that some have ceased.

What follows for design

Paul wrote, “I do not even judge myself … It is the Lord who judges me” (1 Corinthians 4:3–4). Asking can serve discernment, but only God knows a heart. Make results starting points for conversation and service, keep answers private, and never turn a life with God into a score.

In conversation with

  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer, Life Together (1939) placed confession within a community of real people who bear one another’s burdens, not in self-assessment alone.
  • Mark Galli, “Am I Growing Yet?” (2007) asked whether constant monitoring of one’s own growth serves discipleship or turns the disciple inward.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Quizzes that feed a paywall, mood records sent to others, spiritual scores and ranks.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Say what each answer changes, let it be skipped, and protect health and belief data.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Keep answers on the phone and point results toward serving and toward people who know the person, though the app learns less.

Details

Go further

Edge cases to design for

Name the purpose of each question set. For personalisation, ask few questions, say what each changes, allow skipping and editing, and ask nothing about health or belief that the app does not need. For a check-in, let naming be enough, respond with Scripture or a next step rather than a score, and keep the record on the device with a delete option. For gifts, frame results as places to try and people to talk with. For a church survey, keep answers anonymous and aggregated, and share results as a conversation starter. Never send anything automatically, and never rank people.

What tends to go wrong

Quizzes that cannot be skipped and end in a paywall; questions about anxiety, sins or shame without saying why; mood records sent to servers or friends automatically; charts of feelings that invite self-monitoring; results that hand out a label (“You are a Teacher”); spiritual scores, stages and rankings against others; prompts to retake a test to raise a score; leaders seeing individual answers.

How to test it

For every question, ask what it changes and who could ever see the answer, and check that it can be skipped. Ask people to read their result aloud and say what it claims about them, and whether that is fair. Check what is stored and sent with a network inspector. Ask a pastor or spiritual director how they would respond to the same answers.

Faith and interpretation

Self-examination is old Christian practice. “Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith” (2 Corinthians 13:5); “Search me, O God, and know my heart” (Psalm 139:23). The Examen asks people to notice their feelings each evening, and Wesley’s band meetings asked the same questions every week, among friends. Paul wrote that “to each one the manifestation of the Spirit is given for the common good” (1 Corinthians 12:7), and told readers, “Do not think of yourself more highly than you ought” (Romans 12:3). He also wrote “I do not even judge myself … It is the Lord who judges me” (1 Corinthians 4:3–4). Asking can serve discernment; only God knows a heart, and no app should pretend to score one.
