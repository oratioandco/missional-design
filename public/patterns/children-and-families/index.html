Children and families

When children meet Scripture through an app, who is with them, and what does the app ask of them?

When people brought children to Jesus and the disciples turned them away, he “was indignant” and said, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of God belongs to such as these” (Mark 10:14). Children now meet Bible stories, prayers and songs through apps: some made for them, some made for adults, some built to hold their attention, some run by their church, and AI chat apps rated for all ages. Each decides who is with the child, what it collects, how it keeps them watching, and what happens when a child says something worrying. Most of the time, children’s apps help families share the faith. Design also for the frightening story and the worried child.

What we noticed

Takeaways

  1. Design for the grown-up beside the child

    For young children, the best screen is one a grown-up shares. Give the grown-up the story, a few “I wonder” questions, and notes for the hard parts.

  2. Collect nothing a child need not give

    A children’s app needs no account, no name and no age to tell a story. Keep purchases, links and settings behind a parental gate, and say in plain words what, if anything, is collected and for whom.

  3. Switch off what holds attention for its own sake

    Stars, streaks, autoplay and “next episode in 5” keep a child watching. The EU asks for them to be off by default for minors; leave them out.

    Progress and streaks →

  4. Tell hard stories with care

    The Flood, the plagues and the cross are part of the story children are told. Mark the hard parts, give the grown-up something honest to say, and suggest that a child alone talks it over with someone.

  5. Answer a worried child first

    When a child writes that they are scared at home, thank them, give a children’s helpline such as Nummer gegen Kummer (116 111) and suggest a trusted adult. Do not send it automatically to a parent: the person a child fears may be the parent.

  6. Young people: two leaders, consent, a named contact

    A church youth app can ask for no personal details, collect parents’ consent, keep leaders in pairs and out of one-to-one messages, delete the group when the course ends, and name a safeguarding contact outside the leadership.

    Reading together →

  7. A child should not be alone with an AI

    A general AI chat, rated 4+, can answer a frightened child with a verse about obedience. If children can reach it, it needs an age-appropriate mode, a helpline, and no Scripture pressed on a child in distress.

    Sensitive conversations →

The research behind it

Theology, practice and science

Scripture and tradition

Welcomed, taught at home, protected

  • Let the children come

    When the disciples rebuked those who brought children, Jesus “was indignant”; he “took the children in His arms, placed His hands on them, and blessed them” (Mark 10:14, 16).

  • Taught at home

    Israel was told to teach God’s words to their children “when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up” (Deuteronomy 6:7). Timothy’s faith “first dwelt in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice” (2 Timothy 1:5).

  • Wondering together

    In Godly Play, a story is followed by “I wonder” questions and time to respond. There is no right answer to find, and children can bring what troubles them.

    SWikipedia, on the method developed by Jerome Berryman

Safeguarding and law

Protecting children

  • The weight of harm

    Jesus said that whoever causes “one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble” would be better off with “a large millstone hung around his neck” (Matthew 18:6). Churches have failed children badly, and the inquiries into those failures are why safeguarding rules exist. When Scripture is used to harm

  • A children’s app on a phone

    Apple’s Kids Category requires that links out and purchases be “reserved for a designated area behind a parental gate”, and that apps “should not include third-party analytics or third-party advertising”. The UK Children’s Code begins with the “Best interests of the child”.

    SAppleSInformation Commissioner’s Office (UK)

  • Private by default, no streaks

    The European Commission’s 2025 guidelines ask platforms to set minors’ accounts to private by default, to disable streaks, autoplay and push notifications by default, and to put “safeguards around AI chatbots”. The amended US children’s privacy rule requires separate parental consent before children’s data is shared with third parties.

    SEuropean CommissionSLatham & Watkins

  • Young people: in a group, with two adults

    Church of England guidance recommends group calls rather than one-to-one calls with young people, and one parish allows conversations with young people only where at least two DBS-checked adults are present. KonApp’s data protection agreement has the parish collect consent and deletes a group six weeks after confirmation.

    SChurch of England National Safeguarding TeamSAll Saints Boyne Hill, Church of England parishSDeutsche Bibelgesellschaft and each parish or institution

What children’s apps do

    The WHO does not recommend sedentary screen time for one-year-olds and suggests no more than an hour at two, and encourages “reading and storytelling with a caregiver”. Theological reflection

What is this practice for?

What children are for, in the church

Jesus did not treat children as the church’s future but as its present: “the kingdom of God belongs to such as these” (Mark 10:14), and “anyone who does not receive the kingdom of God like a little child will never enter it” (Mark 10:15). Faith is passed on at home, “when you sit at home and when you walk along the road” (Deuteronomy 6:7), as it was from Lois and Eunice to Timothy (2 Timothy 1:5).

Where Christians differ

Churches that baptise infants receive children into the church before they can speak; others dedicate them and wait for their own confession of faith. Catechism, Sunday school, children’s church, Godly Play and confirmation classes teach in different ways. All take seriously that adults answer to God for how they treat children: “if anyone causes one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble” (Matthew 18:6), a warning the church has had to hear about its own failures.

What follows for design

Put the app in the hands of the grown-up beside the child where you can. Ask nothing of a child that a story does not need, and sell them nothing. Let stories end. Tell hard stories honestly, with room to wonder. Answer a frightened child with help, never with a verse that tells them to be quiet.

  • Martin Luther, Small Catechism wrote each part “As the head of the family should teach them in a simple way to his household”: faith taught at home, by a parent, in plain words.

Three ways to build

What would each way do here?

  • ExploitativeTakes from the person

    Collect children’s details for marketing, sell to them, keep them watching with stars, streaks and autoplay, and leave them alone with chat and comments.

  • EthicalDoes no harm

    Ask for no account, no ads and no third-party analytics, put purchases and settings behind a parental gate, switch off streaks and autoplay, and keep youth groups to two leaders.

  • RedemptiveGives something up for them

    Design for the grown-up beside the child, tell hard stories honestly with room to wonder, answer a frightened child with help instead of an automatic message to a parent, and give up the engagement that games and rewards would bring.

Exercise

Hand your app to a child of the age you design for, with a grown-up watching. Write down every place the child could buy something, leave the app, meet a stranger or be asked for a detail. Then read the Flood with a five-year-old and note what the app gave the grown-up to say. Finally, type “I’m scared when my dad shouts” where a child could type it, and follow it: who sees it, what the child is told, and who could help.

Edge cases to design for

Where you can, design for a grown-up beside the child: a story to read aloud with questions to wonder about together. For a child using the app alone, ask for no account and no personal details, show no ads, keep purchases, links and settings behind a parental gate, and leave out streaks, autoplay and notifications. Tell hard stories with care, and suggest talking them over. For young people in a church group, keep conversations in the group with two leaders, get parents’ consent, and name a safeguarding contact. When a child writes something worrying, answer the child: say it was right to tell, offer a helpline for children and a trusted adult, and, in a church-run app, route it to the trained safeguarding contact, not automatically to a parent.

What tends to go wrong

Accounts, ages and names asked of children; third-party analytics and advertising; purchases and links a child can reach; streaks, badges and autoplay that keep a child watching; frightening scenes labelled “KIDS” with no grown-up in view; one-to-one messages between a leader and a young person; a child left alone with an AI chat or a public comment thread; a worrying message sent automatically to a parent who may be the danger, or answered with nothing.

How to test it

Hand the app to a child of the target age with a grown-up watching, and note every place the child could buy, leave the app, meet a stranger or be asked for a detail. List every piece of data collected and who receives it. Read the Flood or the crucifixion with a five-year-old and see what the app gives the grown-up to say. Type a worrying message as a child would, and walk through who sees it and what the child is told.

Faith and interpretation

When people brought little children to Jesus and the disciples rebuked them, he “was indignant and told them, ‘Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of God belongs to such as these’” (Mark 10:14). Israel was told to teach God’s words “to your children and speak of them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road” (Deuteronomy 6:7). Jesus also said that whoever causes “one of these little ones who believe in Me to stumble” would be better off with “a large millstone hung around his neck” (Matthew 18:6). Children are welcomed, taught at home, and protected.
