Missional by Design: principles for spiritual apps

Five places where a Bible, prayer, church or AI app takes a posture, and two commitments in each, derived from the calling to build these tools.

Draft · specialist review pending

Expertise needed: theology across Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant traditions; and pastoral care.

Why, what, how

The calling says why this work matters and how heavy it is. These principles say what we hold, with questions to test a design. The deep dives show how, option by option. Every principle names what it asks the maker to give up: that is the thread through all ten.

Five places, ten commitments

The Word

What do we put in front of people as God’s word, or about it?

  1. Keep the Word distinct, and teach it truly

    Every screen teaches. Let readers always tell Scripture from our words, and our words from a machine’s.

  2. Never speak for God

    An app may quote, explain, pray the church’s prayers and point. It must never pronounce, promise or plead in God’s name, nor let a persona or a machine take the place of a person or of God.

The person

How do we receive the one holding the phone?

  1. Design for the most vulnerable first

    Design first for the person most likely to be hurt: in crisis, grieving, abused, scrupulous, new to faith, hurt by a church, or, where an app serves them, a child. Help comes before features, verses or answers.

  2. Welcome before you ask, and guard what is told in confidence

    Let people reach what they came for before asking anything; ask only for what serves them, when it does; and treat what they tell you as holy ground.

The practice

What do we ask of their time, attention and money, and when do we stop?

  1. Feed the flock, not on it

    Attention, time, devotion and money are entrusted, not harvested. Ask for them honestly, never through guilt, fear, urgency or a promise.

  2. Make room for rest, and say when it is enough

    A faithful product sometimes asks for less: it ends a session, keeps the night quiet, stops asking when the need is met, and lets itself end when its work is done.

The people

Where do we send them, and what do we claim to have grown?

  1. Send people to God and to one another

    The app is a means, never the destination. Judge it by where people go when they close it.

  2. Plant and water; leave the growth to God

    Measure what serves the person and the task. Never score a soul, and never claim the fruit as yours.

The makers

Who are we becoming as we build?

  1. Make it well, as an offering

    Craft is worship. Excellence, accessibility, beauty and care are how we love God and the person on the other side of the screen.

  2. Hold it with joy and trembling, before God and others

    Rejoice in the work and take its weight: pray for the people who use it, invite those who can say no, confess mistakes plainly, and tend what you release.

Three ways to build, read for spiritual apps

The three ways are the work of Praxis, whose Redemptive Frame sets the exploitative, ethical and redemptive ways of building across what we build, how and why. Its redemptive way is “creative restoration through sacrifice”, and sacrifice includes choosing to “withhold a claim” for the sake of another. FaithTech’s Playbook makes the same turn for technology. This guide applies those frameworks to the theological and pastoral responsibilities of spiritual apps. Why we use the frame, and its limits →

The ethical column is not a failure. It is common grace, and many apps never reach it. But FaithTech warns that a product “can be safe and useful while still encouraging isolation, dependency, disembodiment”, and in a faith app the first temptation is not only to use people but to use God’s name. The same craft serves both ends: God filled Bezalel “with the Spirit of God, with skill, ability, and knowledge in all kinds of craftsmanship” (Exodus 31:3), and in the next chapter Aaron fashioned a calf “with an engraving tool” (Exodus 32:4).

How the eight became the ten

Until October 2026 the framework had eight principles: Reverence, Hospitality, Rootedness, Bridge, Grace, Honesty, Costliness and Fruit. Read against the calling and the deep dives, they lacked joy and craft, said nothing about speaking for God, treated the most vulnerable as a qualification rather than a starting point, had no principle for confidences, money or “enough”, and kept sacrifice as one item among eight. Their old addresses lead here.

When principles pull apart

The ten overlap by design. Use the two or three that bear on a decision, and record the tension instead of scoring it. Immediate safety, the law and truthful representation constrain every option; no aspiration cancels them.

DecisionPrinciples in tensionA resolution to test
A reading plan with a daily reminderFeed the flock; Make room for restA rhythm the person chose, easy rescheduling, and after silence one question, then quiet.
An AI that prays with someone at nightNever speak for God; Design for the most vulnerable firstOffer the church’s prayers as the church’s, a way to a person first, and no persona that says it will pray.
A community invitation after an abuse disclosureSend people to one another; Design for the most vulnerable firstKeep the choice with the person, check the destination is safe, and ask a safeguarding lead.
A subscription that pays for licensed notesFeed the flock; Make it wellA price after a first use that says what it pays for, and a cancellation as easy as the purchase.

Use them in a review

  1. Start with a person. Name who is holding the phone, and the most vulnerable person likely to.
  2. Choose the two or three principles that bear on the decision, and ask their questions.
  3. Write the three ways. One sentence each: what would an exploitative, an ethical and a redemptive product do here?
  4. Name the cost. What will we give up, and who outside the team agrees?
  5. Decide, and revisit. Record the tension, the owner and what would change the decision.
