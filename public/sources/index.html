Sources, with their limits.

See what was reviewed, what each resource contributes, and where it informs this reference.

This growing bibliography brings together interaction design, Scripture data, product documentation, spiritual formation, health research and legal sources. Its AI design references include a collection curated in Vitaly Friedman’s LinkedIn resource post. That collection is credited below alongside an expanding domain bibliography. Their depth of review varies: reading a public syllabus is not the same as taking a course, and reviewing an index is not an audit of every pattern.

General principles are adapted into original domain scenarios and wireframes. App examples are presented separately with provenance and attribution. Protected course materials are not reproduced. Read the evidence method before treating a card as a validated recommendation.

Invites people to “Swipe through a quiet, distraction-free feed of Bible verses — one at a time”, each in its own layout and colour, with a random mode. A calm feed is still a feed: decide whether the verses come in order and where it ends.

Limit: A new app with few ratings; the listing only.

Says that from the Verse of the Day you can “read the full chapter the verse is found in”, and that “You can heart, comment on, or share the verse of the day”. The most widely used Bible app puts a route to the chapter and social reactions on its daily verse card.

Social media apps are “deliberately” addictive to users

Social media apps are “deliberately” addictive to users · Hilary Andersson · BBC Panorama, 3 July 2018

Article reviewed 23 October 2026

Aza Raskin, who designed infinite scroll in 2006, says “If you don’t give your brain time to catch up with your impulses … you just keep scrolling”, and that he did not recognise how addictive it could be. An endless feed is built to remove the moment of choosing to go on; give the reader that moment back.

Limit: A designer’s reflection reported in a news programme, not a study.

The loop and reasons to break it: investigating infinite scrolling behaviour in social media applications and reasons to stop

The loop and reasons to break it: investigating infinite scrolling behaviour in social media applications and reasons to stop · Jan Ole Rixen and colleagues (Ulm University, TU Darmstadt) · Proc. ACM HCI 7 (MobileHCI), 2023

Paper reviewed 23 October 2026

Infinite scrolling “has been reported to make users feel like they are being caught in a loop, regretfully elongating SM sessions”; in a one-week field study (N=46), “most reasons to break SM sessions were not related to the app but the user’s general context”. People stop scrolling because life interrupts, not because the feed lets them go; build the stop into the reading.

Limit: Social media apps, a small sample and one week; not Bible apps.

Large meta-analysis links TikTok and Instagram Reels to poorer cognitive and mental health

Large meta-analysis links TikTok and Instagram Reels to poorer cognitive and mental health · PsyPost · reporting Nguyen et al., Psychological Bulletin 151(9), 2025

News report reviewed 23 October 2026; the paper itself was not accessible

Across 71 studies with 98,299 participants, “higher levels of use were most consistently linked to poorer attention span and reduced inhibitory control”; the authors note that “The majority of the studies analyzed were cross-sectional”. Heavy short-form video use goes with weaker attention; a Bible feed that copies the format should not assume it is neutral.

Limit: Correlational, mostly self-reported and about video platforms, not verse feeds; reported through a news summary.

Short-form videos degrade our capacity to retain intentions: effect of context switching on prospective memory

Short-form videos degrade our capacity to retain intentions: effect of context switching on prospective memory · Francesco Chiossi, Luke Haliburton, Changkun Ou, Andreas Butz and Albrecht Schmidt (LMU Munich) · CHI 2023; arXiv:2302.03714

Abstract reviewed 23 October 2026

In an experiment with 60 people, “the TikTok condition significantly degraded the users’ performance” on a task of remembering an intention across interruptions; Twitter and YouTube did not. Rapid switching between unrelated items is what costs attention; cards in order switch less than a shuffled feed.

Limit: A lab study with a small sample, about video, not text.

New features for teens and families on TikTok

New features for teens and families on TikTok · TikTok Newsroom · March 2023

Announcement reviewed 23 October 2026

Accounts of people under 18 get a daily limit; when it is reached they enter a passcode, “requiring them to make an active decision to extend that time”. Even the platform that popularised the endless feed adds a point where going on is a decision; a Bible app can make that point the end of the passage.

Limit: A company’s description of its own feature; its effect was not tested here.

Never read a Bible verse

Never read a Bible verse · Greg Koukl · Stand to Reason

Article reviewed 23 October 2026

“Never read a Bible verse. … Instead, always read a paragraph at least”, because “Meaning always flows from the top down, from the larger units to the smaller units”. A popular evangelical rule of thumb for reading in context; put the paragraph one tap from every card.

Limit: An apologist’s teaching article; one approach to interpretation among several.

Feed pattern

Feed pattern · Web Accessibility Initiative · W3C ARIA Authoring Practices Guide

Reviewed 23 October 2026

A feed is “a dynamic list of articles that often appears to scroll infinitely”, with a contract that lets screen reader users “jump forward and backward by article”. Mark up a card feed as a feed of articles, so screen readers can move card by card and know where they are.

Limit: Guidance for the web; native platforms have their own equivalents.

Privacy as contextual integrity

Privacy as contextual integrity · Helen Nissenbaum · Washington Law Review 79(1), 2004

Abstract reviewed

Proposes contextual integrity as a benchmark that ties adequate privacy protection “to norms of specific contexts”: what is appropriate depends on the context in which information was shared and where it flows. A prayer or confession told to an assistant for one purpose should not flow into recommendations, marketing or another conversation.

Limit: A legal and philosophical framework, not a design study.

“It’s a fair game”, or is it? Examining how users navigate disclosure risks and benefits when using LLM-based conversational agents

“It’s a fair game”, or is it? Examining how users navigate disclosure risks and benefits when using LLM-based conversational agents · Zhiping Zhang et al. · CHI 2024

Abstract reviewed

Analysed sensitive disclosures in real ChatGPT conversations and interviewed 19 users: users constantly trade privacy against usefulness; “erroneous mental models and the dark patterns in system design limited their awareness and comprehension”; human-like interaction encouraged more sensitive disclosure. People will tell an assistant more than they intend and misjudge what is kept; show it to them.

Limit: General chatbot use; small interview sample.

Memory and new controls for ChatGPT

Memory and new controls for ChatGPT · OpenAI · 13 February 2024, updated 2025

Announcement as quoted in press coverage (Decrypt, TechCrunch); the page blocks automated reading

Introduced memory that people can ask to remember or forget, view and switch off, and Temporary Chat without memory; said it would “steer ChatGPT away from proactively remembering sensitive information, like your health details—unless you explicitly ask it to.” Later updates let people switch off reference to saved memories and to chat history separately. The largest chat product treats memory as something to see, delete and switch off, and holds back sensitive categories; a faith app should do no less.

Limit: A company’s description of its own product; behaviour was not tested here.

LLM01:2025 Prompt Injection

LLM01:2025 Prompt Injection · OWASP GenAI Security Project · 2025

Entry reviewed

“Indirect prompt injections occur when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files”; hidden text can change the model’s behaviour or leak data. Treat attached notes, pages and files as material to read, never as instructions, and say so.

Limit: A security catalogue, not a user study.

Code of Canon Law, canons 959–997: the sacrament of penance

Code of Canon Law, canons 959–997: the sacrament of penance · Holy See · Code of Canon Law (1983)

Canon 983 reviewed

Canon 983 §1: “The sacramental seal is inviolable”; a confessor may not betray a penitent “in words or in any manner and for any reason.” The church’s strongest form of confidentiality; an assistant is not a confessor, which is why it should keep confidences it cannot protect as little as possible.

Limit: Catholic canon law; other traditions practise confidentiality differently.

Confessions, Book X

Confessions, Book X · Augustine of Hippo · c. 400 · New Advent translation

Chapters 5 and 8 reviewed

“There is something of man which the spirit of man which is in him itself knows not. But You, Lord, who hast made him, know him wholly” (X.5); “Great is this power of memory, exceeding great, O my God” (X.8). Only God knows a person wholly; a record of conversations is not knowledge of a person.

Limit: A classical theological reflection on memory and self-knowledge.

The End of Memory: Remembering Rightly in a Violent World (review)

The End of Memory: Remembering Rightly in a Violent World (review) · Miroslav Volf · Eerdmans, 2006 · Themelios review

Reviews reviewed

Argues that memory of wrongs must be truthful and that its proper end, after justice and reconciliation, is a kind of non-remembrance, drawing on God’s promise not to remember sins. Remembering rightly includes letting go; an assistant should be able to forget, and let people make it forget.

Limit: Read through reviews; no quotation from the book used.

Gyroscope: AI coach check-ins and the “About You” consultation

Gyroscope: AI coach check-ins and the “About You” consultation · Gyroscope · iOS app 11.1, observed 8 October 2026

Used on a member’s own phone with their permission; no screenshots published because the screens hold personal health data

Each check-in starts with a switch between “Quick chat” and “Save to history”. What the coach knows is kept as a written profile, “About You”, from an onboarding interview: summary, daily rhythm, food and stress, and a section titled “Internal notes” that the member can read. Corrections go through “Discuss with your coach” prompts rather than item-by-item editing. Asked in chat, the coach recited remembered goals, routines and a health condition, but could not say which screen lists them. The profile also noted church attendance, gathered by a fitness app. A per-conversation “keep or don’t keep” switch is simple and clear, and showing even the internal notes is honest. But a prose profile can’t be edited line by line, and a health coach noting church attendance shows how faith details drift into profiles that were never about faith.

Limit: One app, one account; behaviour may differ for other members and versions.
