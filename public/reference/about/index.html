A design reference

Equipping workers in God’s field.

This resource helps everyone who shapes Bible, prayer and AI products to reflect on that calling, to be glad in it and to do it well, so that what we build leads to human flourishing and spiritual nourishment. It gathers reusable decisions, independent of any app, framework or vendor.

Why it exists

People now bring their prayers, doubts, confessions and questions about God to a screen first. When some told Jesus to silence the crowds praising him, he answered, “Listen—if they were silent, the very rocks would start to shout!” (Luke 19:40, The Voice). Making him known is entrusted to us first. But many people now talk to rocks first: a microchip is a rock we tricked into thinking, and with AI the rock talks back. We get to help these rocks reflect Christ and lead people into deeper knowledge of God. Those who build these products plant and water; God gives the growth (1 Corinthians 3:6). That is an exhilarating calling and a real weight. Workers in God’s field sets out the joy of the work and its spiritual, theological, ethical and legal responsibility.

Who it is for

Product designers, product managers, developers and founders who build Bible, prayer, devotional, study and AI products, and the ministry, business and church leaders who guide them. Much of it also applies to other products that hold sources, reflection or sensitive conversations.

How a deep dive works

Each deep dive takes one design decision and lays its real options side by side, from what apps do today to leaving the feature out. At a glance shows a still of every option with one gain and one cost. Try it is a working example on a phone-sized screen, with its settings in one panel. Takeaways, Use cases and Research explain design implications, relevant situations, and the Scripture, tradition, science and accessibility guidance behind each decision. Which fits, real Examples, a Theological reflection and Three ways to build lead to Decide: one question and an exercise for your next review. Go further includes related design decisions and their supporting sources.

Where each option comes from

The option labels identify where an approach comes from and its development status:

  • Observed: documented in a named app, with capture dates shown where screenshots are available.
  • Familiar convention: an established approach used across digital products.
  • Borrowed: an approach adapted from another field, such as reading or productivity.
  • Exploratory: a new design proposal awaiting user testing.
  • Leave it out: choosing to leave the feature out, a legitimate design decision.
  • Warning case: harmful behaviour found in products or research, shown so your team can recognise it.

Use-case tables offer suggested fits based on the research. Original prototypes await user testing. Use the comparisons to develop and test approaches with your audience; assess clinical outcomes and spiritual formation through appropriate research and pastoral discernment.

How sources are used

Every source states what was reviewed, what it contributes, how it is applied and where its limits lie, and the research sections cite them by name. Technical specifications establish meaning; help articles document how a product behaves; empirical studies report findings for the people and systems they studied; legal texts set requirements within their scope. Each source is used within that scope. Research sections identify sources, review dates and translations alongside the findings. App screenshots are dated and keep their original language; working examples, stills and diagrams are our own. The AI design collection curated by Vitaly Friedman, the AI Interaction Atlas and other catalogues are credited in the source register.

Scripture quotations

Articles, reflections, research notes, working examples and diagrams quote the Berean Standard Bible (BSB), a modern English translation dedicated to the public domain in 2023. Every build compares each quotation on the site and in the working examples with the BSB’s published text and fails on any difference; the few deliberate exceptions below are listed in the check with their reasons. Luke 19:40 is quoted once from The Voice for its word “rocks”: Scripture taken from The Voice™. Copyright © 2008 by Ecclesia Bible Society. Used by permission. All rights reserved. Some working examples show other wording on purpose, and say so: the translation comparison sets the KJV, the World English Bible and Young’s Literal Translation beside the BSB; the marking example uses the KJV, where a highlight on BSB words has no exact match; the prayer example reads Compline as printed in the 1979 Book of Common Prayer, which quotes the RSV; and in the progress example a reader’s own note quotes the KJV. Historical commentaries, such as Matthew Henry’s on Psalm 23, quote the KJV they were written on. Each example says which translation it shows.

Accessibility and themes

Pages and working examples are checked with axe against WCAG 2.2 AA, in both light and dark themes, and by keyboard. The site follows your system’s light or dark setting, and the switch in the header remembers your choice; working examples, stills and diagrams follow it too. Screenshots of real apps stay as they were captured. If something does not work for you, please say so.

Publication and maintenance

This is an evolving, independent design reference. Sources, app examples, scenarios and patterns are added and revised as evidence becomes available. Review dates remain visible; legal and support references need fresh verification before product decisions. The Digital Spiritual Formation section integrates selected Kairos manuscript sections and reviewed external findings, with further study review in progress.

About the editor

Tobias Treppmann develops this resource through Missional by Design, bringing product design questions into conversation with Christian spiritual formation. Selected Kairos University research informs the editorial perspective. Aperto and VSB are author-associated projects; their prototypes are identified as such, and inclusion is not independent evidence of effectiveness. Other app examples do not imply endorsement or affiliation.

The aim is a useful, revisable contribution: concrete choices that designers, product managers and independent builders can discuss and test. Explore Missional by Design.

Design values and critical application

This reference connects the Missional by Design framework to its patterns through ten principles for spiritual apps, derived from the calling. They replaced the framework’s eight principles on 11 October 2026; the Christian theological posture is explicit. Values frame choices and questions, while evidence and legal scope require their own review.

Each deep dive asks what an exploitative, an ethical and a redemptive product would do, using a frame from Praxis. Read why, and how we understand “redemptive”.
