Waves of Bible and faith apps

Each generation of Bible and faith technology has served people better than the last, and each has imported the reigning technology of its time. Knowing which wave a feature comes from helps a team ask what it was built to do, and whether that serves the reading and practice it now sits in.

The waves

What each wave gave, and what it carried in

Wave 0c. 1985–1995

Bible software on the desk

Floppy disks and CD-ROMs: Bible-Reader, QuickVerse, The Bible Library, CDWord, early Logos.

+ What it gave

Search, concordances and original-language tools for pastors and students.

− What it carried in

The implied reader was a scholar at a desk; nothing else was imagined.

Wave 1from 1993

The Bible on the web

Bible Gateway (1993), then online and desktop study libraries.

+ What it gave

Free access to many translations, side-by-side comparison, copy and paste for sermon preparation.

− What it carried in

Still a reference for people who already read; the page was a digital desk.

Finding a passage →Numbers, headings and notes →

Wave 2from 2008

Mobile and social

YouVersion among the first App Store apps in July 2008; reading plans, verse images, sharing, streaks.

+ What it gave

Scripture in every pocket, plans that give a rhythm, sharing with friends. Over a billion installs.

− What it carried in

The social-media playbook of the time: streaks, notifications, badges and daily-active targets applied to Scripture.

Wave 3from 2006 / 2018

Audio and contemplative practice

Pray As You Go (2006) and, later, Hallow, Pray.com, Dwell and Glorify; Hallow reached No. 1 on Apple’s App Store on Ash Wednesday 2024.

+ What it gave

Prayer and Scripture through the ears; guided practice, music and silence; a far larger audience than the religious niche assumed.

− What it carried in

New format, same behavioural design: streaks, challenges and engagement targets carried over.

Wave 4from 2023

AI conversation

Magisterium AI, Bible Chat, BibleAI and many others answer questions in natural language; some add character chats.

+ What it gave

A newcomer can ask in their own words and get a response grounded in the text, at any hour.

− What it carried in

Mostly a chatbot added to the existing engagement model; new questions of authority, sources, care and data.

The pattern underneath

The inherited assumption

  1. Engagement mechanics arrived with mobile

    Desktop and web Bibles did not gamify Scripture. From the smartphone wave on, streaks, notifications, badges and daily-active targets came in with the platforms, built for advertising products where time in the app is the goal. Formation asks for depth of encounter, not frequency of opens.

    Practice, habit research and consent →

  2. Format changed faster than philosophy

    Audio and contemplative apps changed how Scripture and prayer are received, but often kept the same behavioural design. Most AI apps add a chatbot to that model, answering on demand rather than accompanying someone over time.

  3. Persuasive by design

    Studies of YouVersion describe its design as persuasive technology that trains particular reading habits, and evangelical builders as the driving force behind the digital Bible. Neither is a criticism by itself; both are reasons to ask what a feature trains people to do.

    Digital formation research →

  4. Two errors to avoid

    Rejecting pressure can tip into pure affirmation: a companion that never challenges is not a spiritual guide. The design question is neither gamified guilt nor empty validation, but grace that still tells the truth.

A parallel strand

Formation first, without the playbook

Some tools never adopted engagement mechanics and still reached very large audiences.

  • Pray As You Go

    Launched by the British Jesuits on 1 March 2006 as a Lent trial: a daily ten-minute audio prayer with music, Scripture and Ignatian questions. Used tens of millions of times a year, with no streaks or badges.

    About Pray As You Go →

  • Lectio 365 and Practicing the Way

    Daily lectio and examen rhythms, and church-based formation courses, that treat technology as something to be formed around rather than optimised for.

    Listening guide →

  • What they share

    A fixed, shared practice rather than a personal score; an ending rather than a feed; the same content for everyone, which keeps them simple and also means they cannot adapt to a person.

    Reading history →

  • What none of them does

    Know the person. The open question for the next wave is whether a tool can remember and adapt with care, as a spiritual director listens, without the surveillance and pressure the earlier waves imported.
