Research

Practice that forms, features that pressure

Regular Scripture reading and prayer are among the oldest Christian practices, and there are good reasons, theological and empirical, to help people keep them. The question for a product team is not whether daily practice matters. It is how a feature can serve a commitment someone chose without turning it into a score, a debt or a performance.

Theology and tradition

Why regular practice matters

Across traditions, Christians have treated regular time with Scripture and prayer as a way of being shaped by God, not as a way of earning anything from him.

  1. A pattern in Scripture, not a quota

    The blessed person delights in the law of the Lord, “and on His law he meditates day and night” (Psalm 1:2). Daniel prays three times a day “just as he had done before” (Daniel 6:10). Jesus rises “while it was still dark” to pray (Mark 1:35). The early church “devoted themselves” to teaching, fellowship, bread and prayers (Acts 2:42). Rhythm runs through all of these; a count does not.

  2. Training, not trying

    “Train yourself for godliness” (1 Timothy 4:7). Dallas Willard built The Spirit of the Disciplines on that image: disciplines are training that makes possible what effort alone cannot. His line, “Grace is not opposed to effort, it is opposed to earning”, holds the tension this page is about. Willard, The Great Omission

  3. Means of grace

    Don Whitney calls the disciplines means by which we place ourselves before God to receive his grace. He also warns that for every legalist he meets, he sees about ten people who neglect the disciplines. A product that only worries about pressure can miss that. Whitney interviewed by Trevin Wax

  4. Habits shape what we love

    James K. A. Smith argues that we are formed less by what we think than by repeated practices that train our desires, including the “liturgies” of everyday technology. Ruth Haley Barton describes a chosen rule of life as structure that supports growth, often summarised as a trellis, not a cage. Bonhoeffer’s Life Together begins the day with Scripture, prayer and song, together and alone. Smith review · Barton · Bonhoeffer summary

For the product: helping someone keep a daily practice they have chosen is a good thing to build. The rest of this note is about how to build it without the app becoming the measure of their faithfulness.

Health and flourishing research

What the research suggests

A large body of research links religious practice with health and wellbeing. Most of it concerns attending services, not reading on a phone, and almost all of it is observational.

  • Observational cohort

    Weekly attendance and lower mortality

    Among about 74,000 US nurses followed for 16 years, those attending services more than once a week had a 33% lower risk of death from any cause than those who never attended. A 2020 study found weekly attenders much less likely to die from suicide, overdose or alcohol. JAMA Internal Medicine 2016 · JAMA Psychiatry 2020

    LimitCommunal attendance, US health professionals; people who attend may differ in ways the studies cannot fully adjust for.

  • Observational cohort

    Private practice in adolescence

    Young people who prayed or meditated daily went on to report greater life satisfaction and were 47% more likely to report a high sense of mission in their twenties. In this younger group, private and communal practice were of roughly similar benefit. American Journal of Epidemiology 2018

    LimitPrayer and meditation were asked together; Bible reading was not measured separately.

  • Global panel study

    Flourishing across 22 countries

    In the Global Flourishing Study, people attending services at least weekly scored 0.81 points higher on a 10-point flourishing composite than those who never attended, and reported more meaning, care for others and volunteering. Harvard Human Flourishing Program

    LimitEarly waves; associations differ by country and are not yet causal estimates.

  • Ministry surveys

    Frequency of Bible reading

    The American Bible Society reports that flourishing rises with how often people use the Bible. The Center for Bible Engagement’s “Power of 4” work, from surveys of tens of thousands of people, reports a threshold: clear differences appear at four or more days a week. State of the Bible 2025 · Center for Bible Engagement

    LimitSelf-reported, correlational and produced by ministries promoting Bible engagement; not peer-reviewed. Widely cited in churches, so worth knowing, and worth citing carefully.

  • Review

    Religion, spirituality and health

    Harold Koenig’s review of over 3,300 studies finds religious involvement more often associated with better mental and physical health than worse. It also names the risks: guilt for not living up to high standards, fear of punishment, and rigid or obsessive practice. ISRN Psychiatry 2012

    LimitStudy quality across the field is mixed; a review by a researcher who leads the field.

What this does not show: no study here shows that a reading app, a streak or a reminder produces these outcomes. Several point to community as much as private practice. Use this to take practice seriously, never to promise health or growth.

Psychology

How habits form, and what breaks them

  1. Missing a day does not undo a habit

    In a 12-week study of 96 people, a new daily behaviour took a median of 66 days to feel automatic, ranging from 18 to 254. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect the process; only very inconsistent practice did. Lally et al., 2010

  2. Showing a broken streak makes people stop

    Across seven studies, people engaged less after seeing a broken streak than an intact one, even when their actual behaviour was the same. The drop was larger when they blamed themselves and smaller when the streak could be repaired. Silverman and Barasch, 2023

  3. Fresh starts help people begin again

    Goal-setting rises after temporal landmarks: a new week, month or year, a birthday. The church year offers its own: Advent, Lent, Easter, a new term. A return can be framed as a beginning rather than a debt. Dai, Milkman and Riis, 2014

  4. Kindness after failure helps people try again

    Students prompted to treat a failure with self-compassion studied longer and were more motivated to improve than those prompted to boost self-esteem. Christians will recognise the shape: “His mercies never fail. They are new every morning” (Lamentations 3:22–23). Breines and Chen, 2012

  5. Practice held as one’s own lasts better than practice driven by guilt

    Research on religious motivation distinguishes practice done to avoid guilt or win approval (introjected) from practice someone personally values (identified). The second goes with greater wellbeing; the first with more anxiety. Features that run on guilt push people towards the first. Ryan, Rigby and King, 1993

  6. For some people, guilt is a symptom

    Religious themes appear in a quarter to a third of people with obsessive-compulsive disorder. “Days behind”, broken streaks and urgent reminders can feed scrupulosity rather than devotion. ADAA

Critical, and constructive

Where the tension lies

  1. Practising to be seen

    “Be careful not to perform your righteous acts before men to be seen by them” (Matthew 6:1), and pray to the Father “who is unseen” (6:6). Public streaks, shareable badges and leaderboards put devotion on display. Making them private by default and opt-in to share is not a technicality; it touches a warning Jesus gave about exactly this.

  2. “Behind” as a verdict

    Catch-up screens and “days behind” turn a plan someone chose into a debt the app keeps. R. Scott Clark argues that daily reading is wisdom, not a command, and that people hurt by “quiet time” legalism need grace rather than more pressure. Clark, Heidelblog 2023

  3. The opposite error is real too

    Paul Grimmond warns against using “legalism” as cover for neglect: “To hear from God is not drudgery, but a profound gift of grace.” Whitney sees neglect far more often than legalism. An app that never encourages anything is not neutral. Grimmond, The Briefing 2009

  4. Counting the app, not the practice

    Reading on paper, hearing Scripture in church or listening in the car leaves no trace in most apps, so the record says less than it seems to. Tim Hutchings shows how Bible apps are designed as persuasive technology that trains particular reading habits; John Dyer traces how evangelical assumptions shaped digital Bibles. Hutchings, 2017 · Dyer, 2023

  5. Formation happens with others

    The early church practised together (Acts 2:42), and the strongest health findings above concern attending services. A private reading app can support that, by pointing to a church’s reading, a group or a friend, but should not quietly replace it.

  6. Begun in the Spirit, finished by the score?

    “After starting in the Spirit, are you now finishing in the flesh?” (Galatians 3:3). A score can slowly become the reason for reading. Watch for that in research sessions: when people talk about the number more than the text, the feature has changed the practice.

Consent

A rule of life someone chooses

The difference between a supportive rhythm and a pressure system is mostly about who chose it, who can see it, and what happens when it is broken.

  1. The person sets the commitment

    What to read, how often and for how long. Changing it is never a penalty.

  2. Tracking is opt-in and explained

    Say what is counted and why. Streaks and counts start switched off, or are easy to switch off and hide.

  3. Reminders keep their schedule

    At the time they chose, with a pause and a stop that work, and never with guilt or spiritual authority.

  4. Accountability needs two yeses

    A reading partner or group sees progress only when both sides agreed, and either can stop sharing.

  5. Count what they say counts

    Let people record reading on paper, in church or by listening, or record nothing at all.

  6. A miss is not a verdict

    No days behind, no broken-streak headline. Offer the next step, or a fresh start at a natural landmark.

  7. Never claim growth

    Describe what the app records. It cannot measure faithfulness, holiness or closeness to God.

  8. Stopping is allowed

    Finishing, pausing and stopping a plan are all choices, and the reader’s notes stay theirs.

In the interface

Serves the practice, or pressures the person?

  • Serves the practice

    Your plan: Luke, five days a week. Next is Luke 15.

    Pressures the person

    You’re 9 days behind. Catch up now!

    The plan is the reader’s; the next step is the only number.

  • Serves the practice

    Welcome back. Pick up at Luke 15, or start fresh on Sunday.

    Pressures the person

    You broke your 42-day streak.

    A miss is not a verdict, and a new week is a natural fresh start.

  • Serves the practice

    Reminder at 7:00 on weekdays, as you chose. Pause any time.

    Pressures the person

    Don’t let God down. Read today!

    Reminders keep the person’s schedule and never borrow spiritual authority.

  • Serves the practice

    Mark as read: in the app, on paper, at church, or listened.

    Pressures the person

    Only chapters opened in the app count.

    Count the practice, not the product.

  • Serves the practice

    Reading with Anna, who agreed to share progress with you.

    Pressures the person

    Share your streak badge with your church!

    Accountability needs both people’s consent; display invites performance.

  • Serves the practice

    Finished, paused or stopped. All of these are fine.

    Pressures the person

    Are you sure you want to give up?

    Ending a plan is a choice, not a failure.

Take into your next review

If someone keeps their practice faithfully but never opens the app, what does your product say about them?

Exercise

List every place your app counts, reminds or celebrates. For each, ask: who chose this, who can see it, and what does it say on the day after a miss? Then ask two people from your church how they would want a reading partner to treat them after a hard month.
